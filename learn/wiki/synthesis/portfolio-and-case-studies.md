# Portfolio and case studies

## In short

A portfolio shows two things: the problems you found and how you solved them, and what someone gets if they hire you. The sources, all Kole Jain videos, agree that a few high-quality projects beat many average ones, that images should lead and text should follow, and that real problem-solving (flows, states, research, results) matters more than polish alone. If you have no clients, realistic self-initiated projects built from a written brief are fine, but one video warns against starting a portfolio while your taste is still changing fast. A case study should open with the problem and a short summary of the solution, show the process as a story, and end with an honest reflection. This is career advice more than design-system guidance; for OpenDesigner it mostly informs how decisions and their reasons get written down and shown [inferred].

## House standards

None apply directly: no non-negotiable source is about portfolios. These house standards cover the same habits in design work:

- `STD-process-review-taste-40` (must): prototypes use realistic, product-shaped content, never lorem ipsum.
- `STD-process-review-taste-52` (should): explain why something feels right or wrong instead of labelling it good or bad.
- `STD-process-review-taste-53` (should): practise by making things, ask the right person for critique, and keep going when early work falls short.

## What the sources teach

### When to start, and what to put in

- The biggest mistake while learning design is to start your portfolio now, because your taste will change over the next one to two months. The presenter's own portfolio from about three years earlier had huge animations and giant text; the current one is calmer [S-L19-043] ([[sources/9WVt1CelBfg-the-stupid-simple-way-to-learn-ui-ux-design-in-exactly-10-minutes|The stupid simple way to learn UI/UX design in exactly 10 minutes]]).
- With no client work, build realistic fake projects: take a unique prompt from a site such as Good Brief or Sharpen Design, check it fits the niche you want (something you care about, where products could be sold), expand it into a full brief (the video uses ChatGPT), gather inspiration, then design with the brief in mind and no lorem ipsum [S-L19-037] ([[sources/59XWYgN00nQ-create-a-portfolio-with-no-experience-or-clients-needed|Create A Portfolio With No Experience (or clients) Needed]]).
- Two or three case studies, or three or four interface-only projects, are enough; put the effort into quality [S-L19-037] ([[sources/59XWYgN00nQ-create-a-portfolio-with-no-experience-or-clients-needed|Create A Portfolio With No Experience (or clients) Needed]]).
- Polished visuals alone don't get people hired. A portfolio of products that solve a problem, with their empty, loading, success and error states and their flows, makes landing a job much more likely [S-L19-044] ([[sources/ADaQuZS04Rc-stop-making-pretty-uis-think-like-a-product-designer|Stop Making Pretty UIs. Think Like a Product Designer]]).
- Dashboards are a staple of design portfolios, but most of them are disorganized and messy [S-L19-047] ([[sources/B7k5rOgmOGY-everything-you-need-to-know-to-build-a-dashboard-ui-in-8-minutes-beginner-friendly|EVERYTHING you need to know to build a Dashboard UI in 8 minutes (beginner friendly)]]).
- Hiring is competitive: ask honestly whether you would hire yourself from your resume and portfolio, and work on whatever makes the answer less than a definite yes [S-L19-040] ([[sources/6CC8lLnqa28-6-things-you-probably-need-to-hear-as-a-web-designer|6 Things You Probably Need to Hear (as a web designer)]]).

### Where it lives and how the site is built

- Dribbble suits interfaces and Behance suits case studies. A Notion site is quick and can feel more personal; a personal website looks most professional but takes the most time and energy [S-L19-037] ([[sources/59XWYgN00nQ-create-a-portfolio-with-no-experience-or-clients-needed|Create A Portfolio With No Experience (or clients) Needed]]).
- Seven habits from professional portfolio sites: a headshot above the fold (a short video goes a step further), a concise intro with your name and what you do, two or three pages at most (a single page with section links works for little content), large full-width images with text secondary, visual case studies, a genuine about page, and personality. They are best practices, and several good portfolios skip some of them [S-L19-064] ([[sources/ToJiXPTNnLY-professional-portfolio-breakdown-why-is-theirs-so-much-better|Professional Portfolio Breakdown — Why Is Theirs So Much Better?]]).
- Visitors see a portfolio before they read it, so the work should lead [S-L19-064] ([[sources/ToJiXPTNnLY-professional-portfolio-breakdown-why-is-theirs-so-much-better|Professional Portfolio Breakdown — Why Is Theirs So Much Better?]]).
- One reworked portfolio hero centers the text, enlarges the name, rearranges the stats, shows mobile screens below and slides them up one after another on load [S-L19-085] ([[sources/ulSOdTgoGeY-awful-to-amazing-web-designs-easily|Awful To AMAZING Web Designs Easily]]).
- Creative showcase shots (plain or glowing backgrounds, skews, collages) are meant for portfolios and social media rather than client meetings [S-L19-041] ([[sources/7cTdCu8HMgM-the-definitive-process-to-present-uis-like-a-pro|The DEFINITIVE process to present UIs like a pro]]). See [[synthesis/presenting-designs|Presenting designs synthesis]].

### How to structure a case study

- A case study has two goals: show the problem you found and how you solved it, and quietly show what a client or employer gets by hiring you [S-L19-083] ([[sources/t7mpEDXzjCg-make-a-perfect-ux-case-study-in-8-steps|Make A Perfect UX Case Study In 8 Steps]]).
- Get the problem statement right first, since a solution to the wrong problem does not matter, and put a short summary of the solution at the top so readers don't have to scroll for it [S-L19-083] ([[sources/t7mpEDXzjCg-make-a-perfect-ux-case-study-in-8-steps|Make A Perfect UX Case Study In 8 Steps]]).
- Research can be simple: a Google Form sent to friends and family, grouped into common findings in an affinity map. Note in the reflection that a more varied sample would be better next time [S-L19-083] ([[sources/t7mpEDXzjCg-make-a-perfect-ux-case-study-in-8-steps|Make A Perfect UX Case Study In 8 Steps]]).
- Build personas from the data and focus on goals and frustrations; the photo and the name don't shape the design [S-L19-083] ([[sources/t7mpEDXzjCg-make-a-perfect-ux-case-study-in-8-steps|Make A Perfect UX Case Study In 8 Steps]]).
- Keep the project and its user flow small so more care goes into each part; shapes and colors can tell step types apart (the example uses orange for decisions, blue for user actions, green for inputs and outputs) [S-L19-083] ([[sources/t7mpEDXzjCg-make-a-perfect-ux-case-study-in-8-steps|Make A Perfect UX Case Study In 8 Steps]]).
- Show rough lo-fi wireframes, test them with a couple of people, tweak and test again, then show final designs large with the reasoning beside them [S-L19-083] ([[sources/t7mpEDXzjCg-make-a-perfect-ux-case-study-in-8-steps|Make A Perfect UX Case Study In 8 Steps]]).
- End with a short, honest reflection on what could be better, what was hard, what you learned and what you left out. Tell it as a story, keep it readable in one sitting (about 10 to 15 minutes) and use chapter-style titles so it can be skimmed [S-L19-083] ([[sources/t7mpEDXzjCg-make-a-perfect-ux-case-study-in-8-steps|Make A Perfect UX Case Study In 8 Steps]]).
- A good case study can be followed from its images alone, with a visual or GIF every couple of paragraphs and the learnings at the end. Case studies matter most in process-heavy fields such as UX design, and much less for graphic designers, copywriters or UI-focused designers [S-L19-064] ([[sources/ToJiXPTNnLY-professional-portfolio-breakdown-why-is-theirs-so-much-better|Professional Portfolio Breakdown — Why Is Theirs So Much Better?]]).
- For freelance work, a short case study lists the services, the problem, and the solution with numbers wherever possible [S-L19-038] ([[sources/5JxUJ1fuyO8-make-one-design-change-to-actually-land-clients-stop-struggling|Make ONE Design Change to Actually Land Clients (Stop Struggling)]]).

### Comparing yourself fairly

- Wall of Portfolios lists each designer's experience and location, which puts their work in perspective and discourages comparing yourself with someone who has 15 or more years of experience [S-L19-073] ([[sources/eeN7yUcIWbw-20-top-underrated-web-design-resources-for-2025|20 Top Underrated Web Design Resources for 2025]]).

## Where they agree and disagree

Between the sources (all from one creator, so agreement is consistency rather than independent confirmation):

- **Quality over quantity:** two or three case studies [S-L19-037], two or three pages [S-L19-064], a small project with a small flow [S-L19-083].
- **Are case studies required?** Central in [S-L19-083] and [S-L19-038]; optional outside UX in [S-L19-064], whose presenter has none on his own portfolio; [S-L19-037] offers interface-only projects as an alternative.
- **When to start:** "don't start while learning" [S-L19-043] and "fill it with fake projects if you have no clients" [S-L19-037] fit together: having no clients is not the same as still learning [inferred].
- **Images lead, but show the thinking:** image-first sites [S-L19-064] and showcase shots [S-L19-041] sit beside the warning that visuals alone don't get people hired [S-L19-044]. The case-study advice to show designs large with the reason beside them joins the two [S-L19-083] [inferred].
- **How much a small test proves:** testing wireframes with a couple of people is recommended for spotting confusion [S-L19-083], while [S-L19-038] says tests that small cannot prove a business result. Different purposes [inferred].

Against OpenDesigner's existing research:

- **Portfolios have a surface type:** Q-scope-06 lists portfolios under `experience` (portfolios, showcases and games), separate from marketing pages, app screens and docs. No Decision Card sets rules for that surface.
- **Writing down the why agrees:** DC-L17-10 recommends a plain-language decision log with the reason and source for every value, so others can explain choices later. That is the same move as a case study's "final design with its reasoning beside it" [S-L19-083] [inferred].
- **Realistic content agrees:** "no lorem ipsum" [S-L19-037] matches `STD-process-review-taste-40`.

## Decisions this informs

Few OpenDesigner questions touch portfolios directly:

- **Q-scope-06** (what the screens are for): a portfolio site answers `experience` [inferred].
- **Q-brand-04** (how lively it should feel): the presenter's move from an everything-moving portfolio to smaller, tasteful animations [S-L19-043] leans toward `hero-moments` over `expressive`; the staggered slide-up of mobile screens [S-L19-085] is one such moment [inferred].
- **Q-img-01** (will the product show photos): a headshot or video of the designer [S-L19-064] is portrait photography, the `portraiture` option [inferred].
- **Q-ref-01** (a site whose structure or quality you like): the portfolios named in [S-L19-064] can serve as structure references, under the identity firewall [inferred].

## Open questions

- Are case studies still expected for UI-focused roles? The sources disagree mildly, and none cite hiring data.
- The counts (two or three case studies, two or three pages, 10 to 15 minutes of reading) are one creator's opinions. Is there any evidence behind them?
- Should OpenDesigner give the `experience` surface its own defaults, for example more expressive motion than an app, or does it reuse the marketing defaults [inferred]?
