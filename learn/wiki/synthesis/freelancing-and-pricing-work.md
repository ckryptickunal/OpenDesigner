# Freelancing and pricing work

## In short

Four Kole Jain videos cover the business side of freelance web design: how to set a price, how to win clients and how to show past work. The pricing advice moves from charging by the hour, to a fixed price per project, to taking a share of the extra money the work earns the client. The selling advice is to find the business problem behind a request for a website (usually more customers or sales), solve that, and then prove it with numbers. Every number on this page is one practitioner's rough estimate (the hourly rates from April 2024 and mostly for the US, the landing-page prices from May 2026), so treat it as a starting point and not a rate card. None of this sets a design-system value; what carries over to OpenDesigner is the habit of naming what the screens must achieve and measuring whether they did [inferred].

## House standards

None apply directly: no non-negotiable source discusses pricing or client work. Two house standards touch the same ground and are worth knowing:

- `STD-process-review-taste-50` (should): a product that merely works is not finished; invest in its look and feel, good defaults and good animations.
- `STD-visual-details-44` (should): decide what not to build, and spend the user's time, attention and trust only on features that pay off.

## What the sources teach

### Three ways to price

- There are three models: hourly, a flat price per project, and value-based pricing, which the video calls the "holy grail". Hourly billing puts designer and client against each other (one wants more hours, the other fewer), and clients prefer a hard number up front, so hourly is only useful until you can estimate a project [S-L19-036] ([[sources/2rtsoM2Dqrs-how-to-price-website-designs-with-specific-numbers|How To Price Website Designs (With Specific Numbers)]]).
- Rates depend heavily on location: about $50 an hour for an average designer in the US against about 12 in India. For people who don't know what to charge in the US and similar countries, the video suggests $20 to $30 an hour after one to two years of designing, $35 to $50 after a few successful clients, and $60 to $100 about a year after that [S-L19-036] ([[sources/2rtsoM2Dqrs-how-to-price-website-designs-with-specific-numbers|How To Price Website Designs (With Specific Numbers)]]). These are the presenter's guidelines, not survey data.
- A project price starts from a break-even cost: estimated hours times your hourly rate, with time added for meetings and revisions. Multiply that by a factor between 1 and 2: close to 1 for a well-known client who will bring more work, higher for a dull one. The worked example turns a $4,000 break-even into $6,000 with a 1.5 factor [S-L19-036] ([[sources/2rtsoM2Dqrs-how-to-price-website-designs-with-specific-numbers|How To Price Website Designs (With Specific Numbers)]]).
- Value-based pricing takes a share of the extra revenue the redesign creates, generally 5 to 10%. A client earning $2 million a year who gains 10% after the redesign would pay $10,000 to $20,000. It only works when the result can be tied to a measurable revenue gain and the client agrees an expected return first; a client with no revenue, or a non-profit, gets a project price instead [S-L19-036] ([[sources/2rtsoM2Dqrs-how-to-price-website-designs-with-specific-numbers|How To Price Website Designs (With Specific Numbers)]]).
- Two things to remember when pricing: work takes longer than you think, and your skills are worth more than you think [S-L19-036] ([[sources/2rtsoM2Dqrs-how-to-price-website-designs-with-specific-numbers|How To Price Website Designs (With Specific Numbers)]]). The same creator estimated four to six hours for the main screens of a gamified app and needed three whole days [S-L19-078] ([[sources/jSxxAFxjxbU-i-spent-a-week-gamifying-apps-this-is-what-i-built|I spent a week gamifying apps. This is what I built]]).

### Sell the problem you solve, not the website

- The need behind a request for a website is usually more customers, clients or sales. Clients mostly judge a site on its looks, but the site's users care far more about whether it works, so design for the users and their tasks [S-L19-038] ([[sources/5JxUJ1fuyO8-make-one-design-change-to-actually-land-clients-stop-struggling|Make ONE Design Change to Actually Land Clients (Stop Struggling)]]).
- In his example, a boutique shop with hundreds of products did not need a prettier site; it needed better search and product categories. A pitch that offers to update and organize a boutique's website with a CMS and modern design techniques names the website as the solution but not the problem [S-L19-038] ([[sources/5JxUJ1fuyO8-make-one-design-change-to-actually-land-clients-stop-struggling|Make ONE Design Change to Actually Land Clients (Stop Struggling)]]).
- Niche on a problem (for example, large catalogues that are hard to navigate) rather than an industry, so the same skill serves clients anywhere that problem appears [S-L19-038] ([[sources/5JxUJ1fuyO8-make-one-design-change-to-actually-land-clients-stop-struggling|Make ONE Design Change to Actually Land Clients (Stop Struggling)]]).
- Add services that fit the client's situation: SEO, conversion rate optimization, content, email, paid marketing or a design retainer. For a site that already gets millions of visitors, conversion work beats SEO, because a small gain in sales at that scale is worth a lot [S-L19-038] ([[sources/5JxUJ1fuyO8-make-one-design-change-to-actually-land-clients-stop-struggling|Make ONE Design Change to Actually Land Clients (Stop Struggling)]]).
- Offer to build the site as well as design it, in code or a website builder, unless the project is complex: the client deals with one person and you keep access to the analytics [S-L19-038] ([[sources/5JxUJ1fuyO8-make-one-design-change-to-actually-land-clients-stop-struggling|Make ONE Design Change to Actually Land Clients (Stop Struggling)]]).

### Prove the result

- Compare the site's analytics before and after the new design ships, with admin or view-only access. Small pre-launch UX tests usually have too few people to prove impact [S-L19-038] ([[sources/5JxUJ1fuyO8-make-one-design-change-to-actually-land-clients-stop-struggling|Make ONE Design Change to Actually Land Clients (Stop Struggling)]]).
- Show past work as a short case study: the services you provided, the problem, then the solution with numbers wherever possible. Showing only the finished website no longer sells future clients [S-L19-038] ([[sources/5JxUJ1fuyO8-make-one-design-change-to-actually-land-clients-stop-struggling|Make ONE Design Change to Actually Land Clients (Stop Struggling)]]).
- Looks and usefulness are not a trade-off: the video cites the aesthetic-usability effect (attractive designs are perceived as easier to use) but gives no source for it [S-L19-038] ([[sources/5JxUJ1fuyO8-make-one-design-change-to-actually-land-clients-stop-struggling|Make ONE Design Change to Actually Land Clients (Stop Struggling)]]).

### What a page is worth depends on its craft

- The same creator rates SaaS landing pages on four levels with rough prices: at level one "you'd be lucky to get $500", level two about $2,000, level three about $6,000 to $10,000, and level four well over $10,000. The jump from good to great is many small details, not one big change [S-L19-072] ([[sources/eMMiLeo_UGI-the-4-levels-of-landing-page-ui-ux-design|The 4 Levels of Landing Page UI/UX Design]]). The prices and scores are his estimates.

### Trust wins clients

- Your face, a genuine story and some personality make potential clients want to work with you: a headshot above the fold (a short video goes further), an honest about page, even a blunt note that the portfolio is out of date [S-L19-064] ([[sources/ToJiXPTNnLY-professional-portfolio-breakdown-why-is-theirs-so-much-better|Professional Portfolio Breakdown — Why Is Theirs So Much Better?]]). For the portfolio itself, see [[synthesis/portfolio-and-case-studies|Portfolio and case studies synthesis]].

## Where they agree and disagree

Between the sources:

- **Price follows outcome:** value-based pricing [S-L19-036] and problem-first selling [S-L19-038] both tie the fee to a business result the client can measure. They come from the same creator, so this is one consistent view, not independent agreement [inferred].
- **Two ways to put a number on work:** one video builds the price from hours and a markup [S-L19-036]; another prices a page by how crafted it is [S-L19-072]. They do not conflict, but neither is checked against market data.
- **How much a small test proves:** [S-L19-038] says pre-launch tests with a few people are too small to show impact, while the case-study video tests lo-fi wireframes with "a couple of people" [S-L19-083]. The first is about proving a business result and the second about spotting confusing screens, so both can hold [inferred].
- **Looks versus function:** [S-L19-038] says clients judge looks and users judge function, and argues for both. [S-L19-072] prices pages largely by visible craft. The two fit if the higher levels also work better, which [S-L19-072] implies through clearer navigation and copy [inferred].

Against OpenDesigner's existing research:

- **Measuring agrees:** DC-L11-20 sets up success metrics for a design system (adoption, a quarterly satisfaction survey) and reports that few teams measure ROI. [S-L19-038] measures a website the same way in spirit, with a before-and-after comparison, but on business results such as sales and sign-ups [inferred].
- **Attractiveness is not proof:** OpenDesigner's guardrails (`skills/opendesigner/references/guardrails.md`, section 7) list "treating attractiveness as usability" among things never to automate. That fits [S-L19-038], which uses the aesthetic-usability effect as a reason to aim for both looks and function, not as a replacement for checking function [inferred].

## Decisions this informs

No OpenDesigner question asks about pricing, and none should: it is not a design-system decision [inferred]. The sources inform these questions indirectly:

- **Q-scope-06** (what the screens are mostly for: persuade, operate, read or experience): [S-L19-038]'s first step, naming the business goal before styling, is the same question. Freelance website work usually answers `persuade` [inferred].
- **Q-gov-05** (what problem hurts most today, and how you will measure whether the system helped): [S-L19-038] finds the real problem first and measures before and after; [S-L19-036] needs a measurable result for value-based pricing.
- **Q-scope-01** (app only, or also the marketing site): the landing-page levels and prices in [S-L19-072] apply when the scope includes `marketing` [inferred].

## Open questions

- The hourly rates date from April 2024 and are US-centric. Do they still hold, and what do they look like elsewhere? No source checks them against market data.
- How do you estimate the revenue gain before the work is done, for value-based pricing? The source says there is no easy way around it.
- The time benchmarks for estimating a project were shown on screen in [S-L19-036] but are not in the transcript, so they are missing here.
- Should OpenDesigner's measurement question (Q-gov-05) offer a business-outcome option, such as conversion before and after, for marketing surfaces? Today its options cover pain, adoption, satisfaction and maturity [inferred]. DC-L19-166 proposes an `outcomes` option (product results before and after) built on [S-L19-038].
