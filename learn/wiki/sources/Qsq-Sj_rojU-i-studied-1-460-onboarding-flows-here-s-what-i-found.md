# I Studied 1,460 Onboarding Flows. Here's What I Found.

## Metadata

- Video ID: `Qsq-Sj_rojU`
- Channel: Mobbin
- Published: 2026-04-16T02:03:06Z
- URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Qsq-Sj_rojU

## Summary

A Mobbin video that reviews 1,460 onboarding flows across 986 apps and websites to ask what makes onboarding good and whether a product needs one at all. It finds that the average app has 25 onboarding screens and that some of the longest flows belong to some of the most successful apps, so it suggests shortness may never have been the point; getting people to the moment they feel the product's value (the aha moment) is. The strongest flows sell the outcome instead of listing features, ask a few personalization questions and then show what the answers unlocked, add delight to long flows, and teach in context with checklists, tooltips and empty-state nudges instead of pop-ups. It also covers paywalls inside onboarding, custom screens before the notification permission prompt, and splitting a sign-up form across screens, citing A/B results for several of these. For a design system this gives concrete onboarding patterns, empty-state and permission-primer components, and a reminder that some products (inspiration libraries, AI chat) may be best with little or no onboarding.

## Key Ideas

- Short onboarding is not the point; the average app in the sample has 25 onboarding screens and some of the longest flows belong to successful apps.
- Good onboarding follows sign up, set up, then the aha moment where the user feels the product's value.
- Welcome screens work best when they sell the outcome by showing the product in action, not by listing features.
- Letting people try the core experience before signing up goes a step further in selling the outcome, and the video rarely sees it in apps with AI features.
- A human touch, such as a founder's note or a CEO video at the aha moment, makes the product feel made with intention.
- The best flows add personalization and make it worth the user's time; only 7% of AI apps personalize during onboarding, and AI tools seem to let the product learn from use instead.
- Allowing more than one answer to a goal question (multi-intent) and a conversational quiz tone both raised conversion in cited tests.
- After a quiz, show what the answers unlocked: a personal plan, a date the goal will be reached, or a home page filled with relevant content.
- 22% of the sample shows a paywall during onboarding; some apps pair it with personalization, a one-time offer, a page of social proof, tailored plans or a playful design.
- Long flows can feel short when there is always something going on, such as animations on loading and verification steps.
- Teach in context instead of front-loading education: reassuring copy, tooltips, live password requirement checks and empty-state nudges.
- A persistent onboarding checklist outperformed pop-ups and banners and stays available after the user dismisses the flow.
- A custom screen before the system notification prompt, optionally previewing the notification, is common and said to raise accept rates.
- Splitting a sign-up form across several screens raised conversions in one case, so added friction in one place can remove it elsewhere.
- Culture matters: audiences differ in how much information density feels efficient, so successful flows cannot simply be copied.
- The flows that stuck brought users to value quickly: sometimes by adding delight to a long flow, sometimes by letting users personalize, sometimes by getting out of the way.
- Some products may not need onboarding at all: when the product speaks for itself (an inspiration library, an AI chat whose first prompt is the value), letting users in fast may be best.

### Decisions it informs

- How should the first onboarding screens sell the product?
  - Product in action: The welcome screen shows the product working instead of a feature list. When: As in Timehop, on both mobile and desktop.
  - Animation on open: People get a feel for what the app does without reading a word. When: As in Runkeeper.
  - Try the core experience first: People use the core experience before signing up. When: As in Elma; rare for apps with AI features.
  - Sign-up screen as a pitch: A copy tweak turns sign-up into a pitch, with logos as social proof. When: As in Superhuman.
  - A human touch: Skip the pitch and feel human, for example a founder's note with a handwritten signature. When: As in One Year and Basecamp.
  - Recommendation: Sell the outcome, not a list of features: the best onboarding screens studied had that in common.
- Does this product need an onboarding flow, and how long should it be?
  - No onboarding, get in fast: Users land straight in the product and find value on first use. When: The product speaks for itself, like Mobbin or AI chat apps where the first prompt is the value.
  - Short and sweet: A couple of questions, visible customization, then a sign-up prompt. When: As in Tide: two questions, recommendations customize, then a sign-up prompt.
  - Long but delightful: Dozens of screens with animation, personalization and a first win that do not feel long. When: As in Duolingo (60 screens before sign-up, including a first lesson) or Bipul (61 screens with animation and a personal plan), which do not feel long.
  - Recommendation: There is no single right length; the flows that stuck brought users to value quickly, whether by adding delight to a long flow, letting users personalize, or getting out of the way. Maybe it all boils down to the product.
- When should people be asked to create an account?
  - First, before anything else: Sign up, set up the account, then reach the aha moment. When: The pattern the video says the best onboarding seems to follow.
  - After trying the core experience: Users feel the value (a first lesson, an AI result) before committing. When: The core experience can run without an account, as in Elma and Duolingo.
  - After personalization: Users answer questions, watch recommendations adapt, then get prompted to sign up. When: As in Tide: two questions, recommendations customize, then a sign-up prompt.
- How should first-time users learn the product? (`Q-pattern-04`)
  - Pop-ups, banners and guided tours: Interruptive overlays that disappear once dismissed. When: The approach Mural replaced; the source does not recommend it.
  - Persistent checklist: A clear list of setup steps that stays around after the initial flow is dismissed. When: Setup has several steps; Mural's six-step checklist raised one-week retention 10% relative.
  - Nudges in the empty state: A blank screen instead shows a small hint in the right place. When: First-use screens with nothing in them yet, as in to-do apps.
  - Reassuring copy and tooltips per step: Dry concepts are explained where they come up, so users feel guided. When: The domain is complex or unfamiliar, as in Cake Equity's equity and vesting.
  - Recommendation: Prefer checklists and contextual nudges over pop-ups and tours: the source cites a retention lift for a checklist and says such small aids make the experience feel effortless.
- Should onboarding ask personalization questions, and what should it do with the answers?
  - Ask nothing, let the product learn: No up-front questions; the product adapts from use. When: Typical of AI tools (only 7% of AI apps personalize during onboarding).
  - Quiz, then show what it unlocked: A personal plan, a goal date or a pre-filled home page makes the product feel like it will work before use. When: Answers change what the user sees, as in Endless, Byte Pal, Brilliant and Speak.
  - Let users customize the look: The app feels like theirs before they start, such as picking a map style. When: As in Focus Flight's map style picker.
  - Recommendation: Make personalization worth the user's time, and show what the answers unlocked rather than just collecting them.
- Should onboarding include a paywall, and how should it be presented?
  - No paywall in onboarding: Users reach the product without a purchase step. When: 22% of the sample shows a paywall during onboarding, so this is the more common choice [inferred].
  - Paywall after a quiz with a one-time offer: The offer is meant to drive urgency right after personalization. When: Used by an app auto-captioned as 'The site'.
  - Social proof page before the paywall: A full page of social proof comes before the paywall. When: Used by Timely.
  - Tailored plan recommendation: Plans are recommended from quiz answers. When: Grammarly; almost a 20% increase in plan upgrades.
  - Playful paywall: The offer is shaped like a flight ticket and the phone vibrates as it prints, so the paywall feels delightful. When: Focus Flight.
- How should the app ask for notification permission?
  - System prompt directly: The operating system pop-up appears with no context. When: Not discussed as a recommended choice.
  - Custom screen first: A screen explains the benefit (for example, reminders to build a habit) before the system prompt. When: Brilliant does this; said to improve accept rates significantly.

### Process

1. Name the aha moment: Identify where users actually feel the product's value (Airbnb: first booking; Netflix: watching a show; Mobbin: saving a screen to a collection) and design onboarding to get there.
2. Decide whether onboarding is needed: If the product delivers value immediately, like an inspiration library or an AI chat's first prompt, let users in fast; otherwise choose how long the flow must be.
3. Sell the outcome up front: Open with the product in action, animation, a try-before-sign-up, or a sign-up screen rewritten as a pitch with social proof.
4. Personalize and show the result: Make questions worth the user's time, allow more than one goal, write them conversationally, then show what the answers unlocked.
5. Keep long flows engaging: Add animation and delight to steps like loading and verification so a long flow does not feel long.
6. Teach in context: Use reassuring copy, tooltips, live form validation, empty-state nudges and a persistent checklist instead of front-loaded education or pop-ups.
7. Prime permissions: Show a custom screen that explains, and optionally previews, notifications before the system prompt.
