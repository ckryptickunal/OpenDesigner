# 6 Things You Probably Need to Hear (as a web designer)

## Summary

Kole Jain gives six pieces of advice for beginner web designers: early designs will be bad and quantity breeds quality, there are far more usable colors than the saturated corner of the hex picker, getting hired is competitive, less is more, design does not need to be complicated, and good ideas come from gathering inspiration before starting. The design-relevant core is restraint: give a landing page one or two things to look at and click, and skip heavy 3D effects and animation when they slow loading or make navigation harder. He contrasts conversion-focused sites (an invoicing site, linear.app, huddle.works) that stay simple with an awareness site (Prometheus Fuels) where heavy scrollytelling fits its purpose. For someone building a design system, this supports defaulting to a calm, focused look and choosing how lively to be based on what the site is for, and it describes a way of working: iterate a lot, step away when stuck, and build a mood board before opening Figma [inferred].

## Key Ideas

- Your first designs will be bad, and that is fine; design is iterative and quantity breeds quality.
- When a design is not working, move on to something else and come back later instead of staring at a blank Figma page.
- When he started, he kept to white, gray and one super-saturated blue and got stuck in the habit of very saturated colors; there is a whole world of other colors to explore.
- Online color pickers suggest many colors; pick the one or two that catch your eye rather than using all of them.
- Nature and artwork are good places to find colors you had not thought of.
- Busy layouts with elements everywhere look appealing on Dribbble but hurt usability, because people get distracted easily.
- You are given the whole screen but do not have to use all of it.
- Big sites like Google and Apple use one call to action and one main thing in their header.
- Heavy 3D effects and animations make memorable sites, but they are not worth it if they slow loading or make navigation harder.
- Sites optimized for conversion can be effective with no crazy effects; heavy scrollytelling suits awareness sites that just want to be remembered.
- Usability is at the heart of everything, and there is value in simplicity.
- Do not jump into Figma with only a sliver of an idea; set time aside to get inspired and build a mood board first.
- Judge your own resume and portfolio honestly by asking whether you would hire yourself.

### Decisions it informs

- How lively should the website be: plain with no effects, a few minimal moving parts, or heavy 3D and scroll-driven animation? (`Q-brand-04`)
  - No crazy effects: Calm, fast and usable; still aesthetic and persuasive (the invoicing site shown, and linear.app with its dark mode and some glassmorphism). When: Sites optimized for conversion, where people should buy or try the product.
  - A few minimal moving parts: Some motion that still gets the point across without massive 3D effects (huddle.works). When: When a little motion helps tell the story without slowing the site or hurting navigation [inferred].
  - Heavy scrollytelling and 3D: Animations pop in and out as you scroll; very memorable, but heavy to build and render (Prometheus Fuels). When: Awareness sites that want to be remembered and do not need visitors to buy anything.
  - Recommendation: Default to simple: usability is at the heart of everything, and effects that slow loading or make navigation harder are probably not worth it. Heavy effects fit awareness sites, not conversion sites.
- How many things should compete for attention in a page's header or first view?
  - One call to action and one main thing: Focused and easy to act on, like the headers of Google and Apple. When: Any website meant to be usable when people land on it.
  - One or maybe two things: Still focused, with a second option to look at or click. When: When a second action is genuinely needed [inferred].
  - Many elements everywhere: Eye-catching variety that appeals to the eye but distracts people and hurts usability. When: Showcase shots on Dribbble or Instagram meant to attract clicks, not usable websites.
  - Recommendation: Give people one or maybe two things to look at and click on and get rid of everything else, because people get distracted very easily.
- Where should palette colors come from?
  - White, gray and one super-saturated color: Simple and safe, but limited to the upper-right corner of the hex picker. When: While first learning design.
  - Online color picker: Surfaces many suggested colors; keep the one or two that catch your eye. When: When you want to explore beyond your usual picks.
  - Nature and artwork: Helps find and rediscover colors you had not thought of. When: When interesting colors feel elusive.
  - Recommendation: Move beyond white, gray and very saturated colors once past the learning stage; explore with online pickers and nature and artwork.

### Process

1. Iterate in volume: Accept that first designs will be weak and make many of them; quantity breeds quality.
2. Step away when stuck: If a design is not working, move on to something else, get inspired, then come back and try again rather than opening a Figma file and staring at the page.
3. Gather inspiration first: Before starting a website, set time aside to get inspired and build a mood board from sites like Dribbble and Behance.
4. Explore options before committing: Do not jump in on the first idea; explore all your options so you start with a full idea instead of a sliver of one.
5. Explore color beyond defaults: Use an online color picker and pick the one or two colors that catch your eye; look at nature and artwork for new colors.
6. Strip the page down: Give visitors one or maybe two things to look at and click on, and get rid of everything else.
7. Check effects against the site's goal: Ask whether the site is for conversion or awareness, and drop effects that slow loading or make navigation harder.
8. Honest self-review: Look at your resume and portfolio and ask whether you would hire yourself; work on whatever makes the answer less than a definite yes.
