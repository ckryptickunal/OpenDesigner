# The Reason Why Some Apps Feel Expensive, But Most Don’t

## Metadata

- Video ID: `SAxKK5fbjbc`
- Channel: Wyatt Feaster
- Published: 2026-09-22T17:00:40Z
- URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SAxKK5fbjbc

## Summary

Wyatt Feaster lists eight habits that make an app feel expensive and premium, and argues that most of them are small and cheap to apply. The habits are about behaviour more than looks: anticipate what the user needs, pace the waiting moments on purpose, add delight in both big and small moments, leave animation out where it would slow people down, keep icons, colors, spacing, fonts and interactions so consistent that nobody notices them, give every empty state a way forward, hide small rewards for people who explore, and write error messages that say how to fix the problem. For a design system this matters because several of these are system-level rules (one icon style, a small set of type and color styles, an empty-state and error-state pattern with a next action, a motion budget) and not one-off screen decisions. The video gives no numbers or exact values; it is a checklist of principles with short app clips as examples.

## Key Ideas

- A premium feel comes from many small, thought-through moments, not from one big visual move.
- Anticipate needs like a high-end hotel does: recommend, pre-fill, and offer the likely next step before the user asks.
- Waiting and transition moments are designed on purpose: how long an animation lasts before the next screen, and what happens on screen during the wait.
- Delight belongs in small moments (a password field) as well as big ones (completing an action).
- Delight is optional by nature; it works because people enjoy what makes them feel something.
- Animating everything feels wrong and slows people down, often at the critical moments; premium apps get out of the way there.
- Consistency done well is invisible; inconsistency is felt even by people who cannot say what is off.
- Mixing a filled icon with stroke icons in one set is a subtle tell of a cheap app.
- Empty states never dead-end: they say nothing is here and offer another route, a first action, or a promoted feature.
- Small unexpected rewards found by exploring make an app memorable.
- Error messages give the steps to fix the problem, not only the fact that something went wrong.
- Prescriptive errors are named as a premium feeling that takes very little effort.

### Rules and practices

- **should** (patterns, all, both products): Anticipate the user's next need and meet it before they ask: show recommendations, pre-fill information you already have, and offer choices such as delivery windows. Why: It removes friction by meeting users where they are, the way a high-end hotel thinks ahead for its guests. [[1:10] It's why we see recommendations, pre-filling information, and delivery windows]
- **should** (motion, all, both products): Design transitional states on purpose: decide how long an animation should take before the next screen appears, and what is shown to lift the wait while it happens. Why: When pacing and timing are thought through, the flow does not feel disjointed, like calm music in a luxury hotel's elevator makes the ride less awkward. [[1:53] how long should an animation actually take before moving on to the next screen]
- **consider** (motion, all, consumer products): Add moments of delight in both small moments (such as a password entry) and big ones (such as completing an action), using things like micro animations or a special transition. Why: They are not strictly needed, but people enjoy an experience more when it makes them feel something. [[2:20] Leverage moments of delight in big moments and in small moments]
- **should** (motion, all, both products): Do not animate everything. Leave animation out of critical, repeated tasks such as entering credit card information. Why: Animating everything feels wrong and slows the experience down, often in critical moments; an animation loading on every entry gets frustrating. [[3:16] animate everything, it feels wrong and it slows the experience down]
- **should** (tokens, all, both products): Keep icons, colors, spacing, fonts and interactions consistent across the whole product, to the point where the UI goes unnoticed. Why: Executed at a high level, consistency is invisible; tons of font styles, tons of colors and elements off by one or two pixels feel wrong. [[3:45] I'm talking icons, colors, spacing, fonts, interactions]
- **should** (iconography, all, both products): Do not mix filled icons and stroke icons in the same set. Why: It feels wrong to users even when they cannot say why. [[4:15] one icon is a filled icon and the other icon is a stroke icon]
- **should** (patterns, all, both products): Never let an empty state dead-end the user: say there is nothing here, then offer an alternative route in the app or a first action such as 'Create your first post' or 'Create your first invoice'. Why: It gives the user a stepping stone to the next part of the experience instead of only telling them they have nothing here. [[4:33] they never dead end users]
- **consider** (patterns, all, consumer products): Consider using an empty state to point to another area of the product you want people to find or upgrade to. Why: The empty moment is an opportunity to promote other areas of the app. [[4:55] use those opportunities to promote other areas of their app]
- **consider** (patterns, all, consumer products): Reward discovery: place small unexpected moments where users find them only by engaging with the app, such as a postcard of the destination after a booking is confirmed. Why: The unexpected moment makes the experience feel special and makes people remember the app. [[5:30] give users little nuggets here and there]
- **should** (content, all, both products): Write error states that tell the user the steps to solve the problem and how to act on it, not only that there is a password problem or that an error occurred. Why: An error that only says something is wrong is like a person who answers a request for directions with 'Nope, wrong way'; the prescriptive version feels expensive for very little effort. [[6:13] but rather they provide them with steps]

### Decisions it informs

- Where should the product animate, and where should it stay still? (`Q-brand-04`) (both products)
  - Animate everything: Feels wrong and slows the experience down, often in critical moments. When: The video advises against it.
  - Animate transitions and chosen moments of delight, stay still on critical tasks: Transitions feel paced and connected, completions feel rewarding, and tasks like entering card details stay fast. When: The video's recommendation for an app that should feel premium.
  - Recommendation: Pick the moments: pace transitional states on purpose, add delight to small and big moments, and leave animation out where the user needs speed.
- Should icons be filled or stroke? (`Q-icon-02`) (both products)
  - One style across the set: The set reads as a whole and goes unnoticed. When: Always, according to the video.
  - Mixed filled and stroke: Feels wrong, even to people who cannot say why. When: The video treats this as a mistake.
  - Recommendation: The video does not prefer filled or stroke; it says not to mix them.
- What should an empty screen offer? (`Q-pattern-04`) (both products)
  - A statement only: The user is told there is nothing here and is left at a dead end. When: The video advises against it.
  - A first action: A prompt such as 'Create your first post' or 'Create your first invoice' gives a stepping stone into the product. When: The user has never created anything here.
  - An alternative route: The screen says there is nothing here and points to something else to do in the app. When: The user has completed everything in this place.
  - A promoted feature: The empty space points to another area of the app or an upsell. When: There is another area the product wants people to find.
  - Recommendation: Always give users an alternative thing to do instead of only saying there is nothing here.
- How should an error message be worded? (`Q-form-03`) (both products)
  - States the problem only: The user learns something is wrong but not what to do, like being told 'Nope, wrong way'. When: The video advises against it.
  - Prescriptive: The message gives the steps to solve the problem and how to act on it. When: The video's recommendation.
  - Recommendation: Prescriptive: it feels expensive and takes very little effort.
