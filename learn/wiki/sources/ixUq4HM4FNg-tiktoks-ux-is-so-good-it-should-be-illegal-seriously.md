# TikTok’s UX is so GOOD it should be ILLEGAL (seriously)

## Metadata

- Video ID: `ixUq4HM4FNg`
- Channel: Kole Jain
- Published: 2024-09-14T00:17:19Z
- URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ixUq4HM4FNg

## Summary

Kole Jain breaks down why TikTok's user experience drove its growth: a minimal onboarding (pick interests, a very short tutorial) followed by an algorithm that learns preferences fast, and a main screen with almost no choices beyond swiping up. He ties this to UX principles: more options lead to worse decision-making and higher cognitive load, the most frequent action should have a huge target that needs almost no thumb movement, the bottom nav bar follows the same idea, and the action icons are lined up on the right side for the 70 to 80% of people who are right-handed. He also covers user feedback, both as an immediate response to any action and, for creators, as likes, comments and shares, with short videos letting regular people rack up millions of views overnight. For a design system, the useful lessons are about mobile interaction patterns, thumb-reach placement, limiting choices and removing friction, although the video is a product teardown rather than a spec with values [inferred].

## Key Ideas

- TikTok's onboarding is just choosing interests and a very short tutorial; the app then learns from what you watch.
- Only 30 to 50% of the first thousand videos shown are based on onboarding inputs; the algorithm is trusted to pick better than the user asked for.
- More options lead to worse decision-making, so TikTok removes options rather than just limiting them.
- The main interaction is a single swipe up, which needs no decision and lowers the cognitive load of the UI.
- The swipe target is almost the whole screen and the thumb barely moves, so the action could not be easier.
- The bottom nav bar follows the same easy-reach idea as the swipe, and the action icons lined up on the right side cater to the 70 to 80% of people who are right-handed; almost nothing on the UI is out of thumb reach.
- Auto-snapping and autoplaying videos remove the remaining friction and aim for absolute efficiency.
- User feedback means every action, such as clicking a button, gets an immediate response showing that something is happening.
- For creators, likes, comments and shares are the feedback, and short videos make it possible for ordinary people to go viral quickly.
- Short-form content sells breadth rather than depth: one second a cat video, the next an analysis of Pablo Escobar's drug empire.
- Behind-the-scenes technology (natural language processing, computer vision) can infer what content is about without relying on user inputs like hashtags.
- Giving creators a free, purpose-built editing tool (CapCut) creates a loop where more users become creators.
- An algorithm is only as good as its content, so TikTok needs a lot of creators creating a lot of content.
- The video's conclusion: TikTok combines short-form, high-quality content, a powerful AI algorithm and the removal of any friction in using the app.

### Rules and practices

- **should** (patterns, all): Limit the choices on the main screen; where the product can learn what people want, remove them so the core interaction is a single action that needs no decision (TikTok: swipe up to see the next video). Why: It's been shown that more options lead to worse decision-making; swiping up requires zero decision-making and significantly reduces the cognitive load of the UI. This only works if the product can learn preferences fast. [Tik Tok not only limits those options they entirely remove them]
- **should** (layout, all): Make the target for the most frequent action as large as possible and as close as possible to where the thumb already rests. Why: How fast an action can be performed depends on the size of the target and the distance to it; TikTok's swipe target is almost the whole screen and the thumb barely moves. Values: almost all the screen, pretty much zero. [how fast you can perform an action depends on the size of the Target]
- **should** (layout, all): On phones, put the navigation bar at the bottom and line up the action icons on the right side, so almost nothing on the UI is out of thumb reach. Why: The bottom nav bar follows the same idea as the swipe (large target, little thumb travel), and right-side icons cater to the 70 to 80% of the population that is right-handed. Values: 70 to 80%. [same idea for the bottom navbar but also the icons lined up on the right side]
- **should** (patterns, all): Give an immediate visible response whenever the user takes an action, such as clicking a button, to show the product is doing something. Why: User feedback is an important UX principle: the immediate response shows the user that the website is doing something. [there should be an immediate response to show that the website is doing something]
- **consider** (patterns, all): Keep onboarding to a quick interest pick and a very short tutorial, then learn preferences from behaviour instead of asking for more input. Why: The algorithm can give content better than the user requested; only 30 to 50% of the first thousand videos rely on onboarding inputs. Values: 30 to 50%. [all you do is choose your interest get the shortest tutorial ever]
- **consider** (patterns, all): In a full-screen content feed, auto-snap each item into place and autoplay it so moving to the next item needs no extra action. Why: Auto-snapping and autoplay remove friction and aim for absolute efficiency. [when you consider the Auto snapping and autop playing videos]
- **consider** (patterns, all): Where the product can infer what users want (for example from content analysis), prefer that over making users supply inputs such as hashtags or preference pickers. Why: Behind-the-scenes technology creates a seamless experience; TikTok's added preference page is not really hitting the mark because the algorithm is already good. [never underestimate what tech behind the scenes does]
- **should** (patterns, all): When users create or upload content, give them feedback on it (in social products: likes, comments and shares). Why: This is the social media equivalent of user feedback, and the chance for regular creators to go viral is an important part of the creation-side user experience. ### Decisions it informs

- How many choices should the main screen of the app give people?
  - Many visible options: Lots of buttons and paths; the video compares it to remotes people cover with tape, and says more options lead to worse decisions. When: Not recommended in the video for the core, most-used screen [inferred].
  - One core action, other options removed: People only swipe up; zero decision-making and much lower cognitive load, but it depends on the product learning preferences fast. When: When the product can learn what each person wants quickly, as TikTok does with its algorithm.
  - Recommendation: Remove options so there is one core action; more options lead to worse decision-making and a single swipe reduces cognitive load. This only works if the product learns preferences fast.
- Where should the navigation menu and frequent actions sit on a phone? (`Q-layout-04`)
  - Bottom nav bar plus right-side action icons: Everything sits within a right-handed thumb's reach; almost nothing is out of reach. When: Phone apps used one-handed, especially feeds with a single dominant gesture.
  - Controls away from the thumb, such as at the top: Longer thumb travel to reach actions, which the video's target-distance argument says slows them down [inferred]. When: Not discussed in the video [inferred].
  - Recommendation: Bottom nav bar (same large-target, short-distance idea as the swipe) and icons lined up on the right side, which cater to the 70 to 80% of people who are right-handed.
- How should the app learn what content a new user wants?
  - User-curated feed: The feed shows only what the user chose, such as accounts they follow (Instagram's home feed until recently). When: When users are expected to build their own feed.
  - Preference collage or picker: Users pick preferred content from a grid up front (Instagram, Pinterest); TikTok's recent version is said not to be hitting the mark. When: When the recommendation system cannot learn quickly on its own [inferred].
  - Quick interest pick, then learn from behaviour: Minimal setup (interests plus the shortest tutorial); the system then chooses most content, with only 30 to 50% of the first thousand videos based on onboarding inputs. When: When the product can analyse content and behaviour well enough to predict what people want.
  - Recommendation: TikTok's approach: a quick interest pick and short tutorial, then let the algorithm learn, because it can give content better than users even requested.
- Should the product's content be short-form or long-form?
  - Long-form: Depth of information or entertainment (YouTube averages 11.7 minutes); a bigger commitment to view. Instagram's IGTV attempt was sunset in 2022. When: When depth is the selling point, as for YouTube.
  - Short-form: Breadth: one video can be a cat, the next an analysis of a drug empire; averages 37 seconds, is low commitment, and helps inexperienced creators go viral. When: When breadth, high volume and quick engagement are the goal [inferred].
  - Recommendation: The video credits short-form content as TikTok's secret, alongside its algorithm.
