# Make A Perfect UX Case Study In 8 Steps

## Metadata

- Video ID: `t7mpEDXzjCg`

## Summary

Kole Jain breaks a UX case study into eight steps: define the problem with a short solution snapshot, make the case study look good, build data-backed user personas, map user flows, show lo-fi wireframes, present the final design with the reasoning beside it, reflect honestly, and add style through story, brevity and chapter-like titles. He walks through Emily Shen's portfolio case study as the running example. He says a case study has two goals: to show what problems you found and how you solved them, and to show what someone gets if they hire you. It matters to someone building a design system mainly as guidance on documenting and presenting design decisions and their reasons, not on choosing token values [inferred].

## Key Ideas

- A case study should show the problem you found and how you solved it, and quietly show what a client or employer gets by hiring you.
- Get the problem statement right first: if the problem is wrong or does not exist, the solution does not matter.
- Put a brief summary of the solution at the top, so readers see problem and solution without scrolling to the bottom.
- Research does not need to be hard: a Google Form sent to friends and family works, and an affinity map turns the data into common findings.
- How the case study looks matters as much as what it says; concise text with lots of images and GIFs keeps people reading.
- Charts need clear titles, axis titles and a legend, and circling and annotations help understanding.
- A user persona exists to consolidate data and inform decisions, so focus on goals, frustrations, behaviour and pain points, not the photo or name.
- Keep projects and user flows small; a smaller project lets you put more quality into it.
- Lo-fi wireframes let you ideate rapidly and test assumptions with a couple of people before building a full prototype.
- Show final designs large, with text beside each one explaining why you made those choices.
- A reflection section that admits flaws, lessons and features left out makes the case study more genuine and you a more credible designer.
- Tell the process as a story, keep it readable in one sitting, and use chapter-style titles so it can be skimmed.

### Decisions it informs

- Where should the solution appear in a case study?
  - Brief solution summary at the top: Readers see the problem and then instantly see the solution; the process follows below. When: Always, for clients or employers who skim.
  - Solution revealed only at the end: Readers have to scroll all the way to the bottom to learn the outcome. When: Not recommended by the source.
  - Recommendation: Put a brief solution summary up top, because clients and employers don't want to scroll to the bottom to find it.
- How big should the project and its user flow be?
  - Small project, small user flow: Fewer screens, each with more care and quality. When: Default; you don't need a big project for a good case study.
  - Large project, giant user flow: Massive case studies with giant user flows, and less quality can go into each part. When: Not recommended by the source.
  - Recommendation: Keep it small; the smaller it is, the more quality you can put into it.
- How should step types be told apart in a user flow diagram?
  - Color and shape coding: Orange marks a decision, blue a process the user does, green an input or output, so the flow reads at a glance. When: Kole's favoured approach, mocked up in FigJam.
  - Uniform shapes: All steps look the same, so types must be read from labels [inferred]. When: Not discussed; the source only recommends differentiation.
  - Recommendation: Use different shapes and colors to differentiate steps.
- How should low-fidelity wireframes be made and shown?
  - Hand-drawn sketches: Rough drawings with arrows and shading; fast to ideate. When: If you prefer to draw, as Kole does.
  - Lo-fi wireframes in Figma: Simple digital layouts. When: If you prefer working in Figma.
  - Recommendation: Either works; include preliminary or even incomplete sketches because they add value and show your process.
- How should final design screens be presented?
  - Large screens with explanatory text beside each: Detail stays visible and the reasoning sits right next to the design; clickable tabs can group screens. When: Default for the final design section.
  - Many screens squished together: Detail is lost, so readers can't understand the design decisions. When: Avoid.
  - Recommendation: Show designs as big as reasonably possible with text next to them explaining the choices.
- What kind of section titles should a case study use?
  - Chapter-style titles: Titles summarize the section and make readers intrigued to read more. When: Recommended.
  - Plain, boring titles: Sections are labelled but don't pull the reader in. When: Not recommended by the source.
  - Recommendation: Take inspiration from book chapter titles.
- What should a user persona contain?
  - Data-backed, problem-specific persona: Goals, frustrations, behaviour, interests and pain points tied to the problem and solution; it informs the design. When: Recommended.
  - Detailed persona with photo, name and extraneous details: Looks fantastic but ultimately has no bearing on the final design and wastes time. When: Not recommended by the source.
  - Recommendation: Build the persona from your data and focus on goals and frustrations as they relate to your problem and solution; don't get caught up on the photo or name.
- How long should a case study be?
  - Short, readable in one sitting: About 10 to 15 minutes of reading, with text limited in favour of videos and images. When: Recommended.
  - Long and text-heavy: Every detail written out; people don't like to read, so fewer will get through it. When: Not recommended by the source.
  - Recommendation: Keep it short: concision is your friend, and it should be readable in one sitting, around 10 to 15 minutes.

### Process

1. Set the two goals: Aim to show the problems you identified and how you solved them, and to show what people get if they hire you; every later step serves these.
2. 1. Define the problem and snapshot the solution: Write a clear, concise problem statement backed by research, with a brief solution summary up top. Collect data (a Google Form to friends and family is enough), group it in an affinity map, and show summarized findings.
3. 2. Make it look good: Keep text concise and use many images and GIFs. Present data with clear graphs, circling and annotations, and give charts titles, axis titles and a legend.
4. 3. Build user personas: Make the persona from your data, focused on goals, frustrations, behaviour, interests and pain points tied to the problem; skip time on photo, name and extraneous details.
5. 4. Map user flows: Mock the flow up (Kole uses FigJam) with shapes and colors for decisions, processes and inputs or outputs, and keep it small.
6. 5. Sketch lo-fi wireframes and test them: Draw or use Figma to ideate layouts quickly, include rough sketches in the case study, have a couple of people go through them, observe what is intuitive, tweak and test again.
7. 6. Show the final design and the problems it solves: Show designs large with explanatory text beside them (clickable tabs are one layout) explaining why the design solves the problem best.
8. 7. Reflect: Briefly cover what could be better, difficulties, what you learned, research-method improvements, and features left out for time.
9. 8. Add style: Tell it as a story, keep it short enough to read in 10 to 15 minutes, and make it skimmable with chapter-like titles.
