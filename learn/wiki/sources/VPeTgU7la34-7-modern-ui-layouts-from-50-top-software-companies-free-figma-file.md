# 7 Modern UI Layouts from 50 Top Software Companies (+ Free Figma File)

## Summary

Kole Jain looks at 50 landing pages, mostly software sites (the description admits a few are not), and pulls out seven recurring section layouts, then rebuilds each one in a Figma file offered as a free download. The seven are: a customer logo strip, a hero with big text and a big image, a tabbed 'clickable multisection', simple and tasteful hover animations, a 'simple bento' grid, a 'straight line grid' with visible grid lines, and images inside the navigation menu. He names real sites that use each one and gives practical tips, such as a plain or a marquee version of the logo strip, designing every tab before handoff, and keeping bento grids simple so developers can build them. For a design system, the video works as a catalogue of marketing-page section patterns with options that could be offered as visual samples, plus a steady push toward simple, well-built motion over heavy graphics [inferred].

## Key Ideas

- Most software landing pages share the same few section layouts, so the job is to use them well and add a distinctive touch.
- Customer logos are the most common section: 41 of the 50 sites showed well-known logos as social proof.
- A logo strip can be a simple static row or a marquee that slides the logos across, with a gradient and progressive blur at the edges.
- The standard hero is big text, a big image and lots of space, which pulls people into scrolling for more.
- When everyone uses the same hero, set yours apart with a distinctive font, an inline diagram, an interactive product image or a subtle background gradient.
- A tabbed 'clickable multisection' can switch automatically on a timer or use a simpler layout; whichever you pick, design every tab before handoff.
- Software sites are moving away from heavy graphics toward simple, tasteful animations, and those animations have to be done well.
- An imported third-party effect can look blurry and give itself away; simple hover effects built with care look better.
- A 'simple bento' built on plain grids gives the bento trend without the build cost of an Apple-style bento.
- A 'straight line grid' keeps visible grid lines between feature cells instead of floating cards.
- Images in the navigation menu give people context at a glance before they click, which beats plain lists of links.
- People don't pay attention for long and don't read all the copy, so adding images to the nav makes their life easier.

### Rules and practices

- **consider** (patterns, web): Consider a customer logo section on a software landing page, using well-recognized logos as social proof. Why: 41 of the 50 sites had one, and visitors automatically assume the logos are important companies that use the product. [41 of the 50 softwares I looked at]
- **consider** (patterns, web): For a plain logo strip, lay only your most important logos across the screen, without animation. Why: It is the simple way, and the one the presenter likes. [take your most important logos throw them across the screen]
- **consider** (motion, web): For a fancier logo strip, slide the logos across the screen left to right in a marquee and fade them out at the edges with a gradient plus a progressive blur. Why: The presenter says that if you are getting fancy, and you probably should, this is the version to build. Values: left to right. [the bougie one where we would have a marquee animation]
- **should** (layout, web): Build the hero with big text, a big image and generous space around them. Why: This makes people start scrolling for more information without realising it. [big text Big Image lots of space]
- **should** (layout, web): Give a standard hero a distinctive element, such as a distinctive font, a small inline diagram or an interactive product image. Why: When everyone uses the same layout, you need to be more creative to set yourself apart. [get a little more creative to differentiate ourselves]
- **consider** (color, web): Consider a very subtle gradient behind the hero rather than a flat background. Why: Without the gradient the hero becomes more bland and boring. [I added a very subtle gradient here]
- **should** (process, web): When designing a tabbed section (clickable multisection), design the content of every tab before handing off to a developer or client. Why: Otherwise the developer or client receives it with half the site missing. [make sure to design all the tabs]
- **consider** (components, web): If you don't want the extra work, use a simpler tabbed layout like MongoDB's instead of automatic switching with a timer like Webflow's. Why: Automatic switching with a timer is more complex; if you don't want to do all of that, a MongoDB-style layout is probably a little better. [something like mongod DBS layout is probably a little better]
- **should** (motion, web): Use simple, tasteful animations on software marketing pages instead of heavy, render-intensive graphics. Why: Modern software designs are shifting away from heavy graphics, and software sites typically prefer simple, tasteful animations. [softwares typically prefer simple and tasteful animations]
- **should** (motion, web): Don't ship an animation that renders blurry, such as a scroll effect imported from a third party; build each animation cleanly. Why: The blur gives away that it was imported, and the key is to actually do these animations well. [slightly blurry on my screen]
- **should** (motion, web): Keep hover effects simple and well built; don't go overboard with them. Why: Simple and well put together is always better. [don't go overboard don't make it crazy]
- **should** (layout, web): Build bento sections on simple grids (a 'simple bento') rather than a complex Apple-style bento. Why: Complex bentos are very hard to put together, while simple grids keep developers happy and still follow the trend. [sticking to some pretty simple grids so our Dev doesn't rip us a new one]
- **consider** (layout, web): Consider one slightly tilted card inside a simple bento. Why: Ramp's bento, the presenter's favourite, does this. [ramps Bento that has a slightly tilted card]
- **consider** (layout, web): Consider a straight line grid for feature blocks: keep visible grid lines between cells instead of letting them float apart. Why: The presenter sees it more and more, for example on Vercel's six-section block; Qatalog pairs its line grids with tasteful animations. [what I've termed the straight line grid]
- **should** (components, web): Add images to navigation dropdown menus instead of showing only lists of links. Why: People skim and rarely read copy, and images give context about where a link goes at a glance before they click. [images in the Nav]
- **consider** (components, web): Even on a small website, use a small image menu (like Pipe's); most SaaS sites use giant image dropdowns (like Intercom's). Why: Pipe's small image menu looks really professional and works well. [even if you've got a small website]

### Decisions it informs

- How should the customer logo strip look?
  - Simple static strip: Your most important logos laid across the screen, and you're good to go. When: You want the simple way; the presenter's own preference.
  - Marquee with edge fade: Logos slide across the screen, and a gradient plus progressive blur fades them out at the edges. When: You want a fancier, moving section; the presenter says you probably should.
  - Recommendation: Both are offered. The presenter likes the simple row but says getting fancy with the marquee is probably worth it.
- How should the page show social proof: customer logos, or a direct comparison with competitors?
  - Well-known customer logos: Visitors assume important companies use the product. When: The most common choice (41 of 50 sites).
  - Competitor call-out through a testimonial: A customer quote does the comparing for you. When: You want to position against rivals without saying it yourself (Coda).
  - Direct competitor call-out: The page openly tells people to drop other tools. When: You want a blunt, confident tone (Basecamp).
- How should the hero be aligned?
  - The common hero layout: The layout shared by dozens of software sites, with big text, a big image and lots of space; likely centered, since the source contrasts it with left-aligned heroes [inferred] When: You want the familiar, proven structure.
  - Left-aligned text: Hero text lined up on the left, as on Linear. When: An alternative the presenter left out of his count (Linear); choosing it to stand apart is [inferred].
  - Recommendation: The source does not choose between them; it explains the common layout as big text, a big image and lots of space, then says to add a distinctive touch.
- How do you make a standard hero stand out?
  - Distinctive font and inline diagram: A nifty, characterful typeface and a small inline diagram in the hero. When: You want personality without changing the layout (Magier).
  - Interactive product image: The full-size hero image responds to the visitor. When: You can invest in interaction (ClickUp).
  - Subtle background gradient: Removes the bland, flat feel of a plain background. When: When the hero looks flat; the presenter uses it on his own hero.
  - Recommendation: Get a little creative to set yourself apart, since everyone uses the same layout.
- How should a tabbed feature section (clickable multisection) behave?
  - Automatic switching with a timer: Tabs advance by themselves on a timer. When: You want a more complex, self-running section (Webflow).
  - Simpler layout: A simpler tabbed section without automatic switching. When: You don't want all that extra work (MongoDB).
  - Horizontal or vertical tab layout: Horizontal and vertical versions of the section. When: Either works; Miro uses both, four times on one page.
  - Recommendation: The simpler MongoDB-style layout is probably better if you don't want the extra work, and design every tab either way.
- How complex should a bento section be? (`Q-layout-05`)
  - Complex bento (Apple-style): Rich, irregular tiles; very hard to put together. When: Marketing purposes, per the source.
  - Simple bento: Tiles on plain grids that still read as a bento; easy for developers. When: Software sites (Butter, Kit).
  - Simple bento with a tilted card: One slightly tilted card breaks the grid for interest. When: You want a little more character (Ramp).
  - Recommendation: Use a simple bento on simple grids so it can be built, while still following the trend.
- Should feature cells float apart, or sit in a grid with visible lines? (`Q-dir-04`)
  - Floating cells: Separate blocks floating apart on the page. When: The approach the line grid replaces.
  - Straight line grid: Cells share visible grid lines, like Vercel's six-section block or Qatalog. When: You want the structured look the presenter sees more and more, often with tasteful animations.
- What should navigation dropdown menus show?
  - Plain lists of links: Text only; people don't know where a link goes until they click. When: Not recommended by the source.
  - Giant image dropdown: A large menu with images, as on Intercom. When: What most SaaS sites have (Intercom).
  - Small image menu: A compact menu with images that looks professional. When: Even a small website (Pipe).
  - Recommendation: Add images to the nav: they are more appealing and give context at a glance, and the presenter expects this 2025 trend to stick.
- How much visual effect should a software marketing page use?
  - Heavy graphics: Render-intensive visuals that the presenter mocks as needing an expensive GPU. When: Not recommended by the source.
  - Simple, tasteful animations: Simple hover effects, done cleanly. When: What software sites typically prefer (Welcome, Pipe, Klaviyo).
  - Recommendation: Simple, tasteful animations, done well; don't go overboard.

### Process

1. Collect many real references: Look through a large set of comparable sites (here 50 software landing pages) and note which sections keep recurring.
2. Note what recurs and why: Note how often a section appears (41 of 50 sites had logos) and try to get to the bottom of why these sites look better than your own designs.
3. Make your own interpretation: Build your own version of each layout (for the logo strip, both a simple and a fancier version) and add a distinctive touch so it doesn't look like everyone else's.
4. Design every state before handoff: Fill in every tab and section so the developer or client doesn't receive a design with half the site missing.
