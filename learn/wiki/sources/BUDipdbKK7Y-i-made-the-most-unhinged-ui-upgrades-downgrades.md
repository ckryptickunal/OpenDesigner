# I Made The Most UNHINGED UI Upgrades (downgrades?)

## Metadata

- Video ID: `BUDipdbKK7Y`
- Channel: Kole Jain
- Published: 2024-07-30T01:40:30Z

## Summary

Kole Jain redesigns common apps (YouTube, Amazon, Gmail, a fitness app, Uber, Figma, Instacart, Apple Books, a recipe site and a smart home app) with deliberately passive-aggressive or infuriating features, as a comedy piece. Most of the ideas are anti-patterns: usage-shaming pop-ups with an upsell, locking Gmail to one browser unless you watch an ad, a modal that blocks everything until you name a layer, paywalls or account walls on a simple recipe, and punishments for breaking a reading streak. Even while being annoying, he keeps one good-UX habit in three of them (the Figma naming modal, the Apple Books streak punishment and the smart home pairing): a notice, a button or a setting to turn the feature off. He also points out where a joke idea could turn useful, such as a driver chattiness rating, or a one-click cart if it were based on past orders and showed a cart total. For someone building a design system, the video works mainly as a list of patterns to avoid and a reminder that any forcing feature needs an escape hatch [inferred].

## Key Ideas

- Endless pop-ups, paywalls that block content and pointless premium subscriptions can make websites annoying as hell.
- A message that shames users about their usage, then offers only an upsell or more notifications, might help someone controlling screen time but mostly just made the presenter angry.
- Guilt-tripping copy can slide from passive-aggressive to simply aggressive; the presenter deliberately held back.
- Suggesting that a struggling user lower their goal makes a guilt pop-up even worse.
- Being annoying is no excuse to ignore good UX: tell users how to turn an intrusive feature off.
- A modal that blocks every other action until a chore is done (naming a layer) is something designers would despise about a minute into using it.
- Three of the hostile features come with a way out: the optional Figma naming feature has a notice on how to turn it off, the Apple Books streak punishment has a button to turn it off, and smart home pairing can be turned off in settings.
- A random one-click cart is useless; basing it on past orders and showing a cart total could make it useful.
- Some joke ideas turn out useful, like rating an Uber driver's chattiness so riders can plan the trip.
- Requiring a subscription or an account for something as simple as a recipe would make the presenter mad.
- Punishing a broken streak (making readers guess where they were in a book) is presented as a feature users will hate.
- Letting other people control your smart home devices needs an off switch in settings, and would be far worse for smart locks.

### Rules and practices

- **should** (patterns, all): Do not fill a site with endless pop-ups, paywalls in front of content or premium subscriptions that add nothing. Why: The video opens by naming these as what makes websites annoying. [endless pop-ups pay walls blocking or content]
- **should** (patterns, all): Give every intrusive feature a visible way to turn it off: a button beside it or a switch in settings. Why: Even the deliberately annoying features keep this piece of good UX; the presenter expects users to hate them. [Apple Books: an even handier button below that to turn this feature off; Smart Home: we need to be able to turn this off in settings]
- **should** (components, all): When a feature blocks the user with a modal, include a notice in it that explains how to turn the feature off. Why: Being annoying does not mean ignoring good UX. [Figma: I added a notice here to let the user know how to turn it off]
- **should** (components, all): Do not use a modal that blocks every other action until the user finishes a chore such as naming a layer. Why: It is presented as infuriating; designers would despise it about a minute into using it. [this model that won't let you do anything else until you name it]
- **should** (content, all): Do not shame users about how much they use the product (videos watched, time spent browsing), especially when the only choices offered are an upsell or continued notifications. Why: The YouTube message could help someone controlling screen time but mostly just made the presenter angry; the Amazon browsing-time pop-up is another of the deliberately infuriating changes. [you can either subscribe to YouTube premium or keep getting these notifications]
- **should** (content, all): Do not write guilt-tripping or passive-aggressive messages about a user's progress, such as telling them they would need to run a marathon today to hit their step goal. Why: The video uses this tone on purpose to make apps infuriating; the presenter held back from adding more because it would go from passive-aggressive to simply aggressive. [you'd need to run a marathon today to hit your goal]
- **should** (patterns, all): Do not pair a progress warning with a button that suggests the user change their goal. Why: The presenter says this would make the fitness pop-up even worse. [adding a button to change the goal and suggesting that they do so]
- **should** (platforms, web): Do not lock a web app to one browser or make users watch an ad to get access. Why: It is one of the imagined changes designed to make users mad. [an option to watch a minute long ad to get access to your Gmail]
- **should** (patterns, all): Do not put a paywall or an account requirement in front of simple content such as a recipe. Why: The presenter says requiring a subscription just to make a meal would make him mad. [restricted with either a pay wall or requiring you to make an account]
- **consider** (patterns, all): Base one-click shortcuts, like an auto-filled cart, on the user's past behaviour rather than randomness, and show a running total. Why: A random cart is entirely useless; one based on past orders might be useful, and a cart total helps people keep on budget. [Instacart: if this was based on your past orders ... add a cart total to help you keep on budget]
- **should** (patterns, all): Do not punish users for breaking a streak, for example by making them guess where they were in a book. Why: It is presented as a punishment feature the presenter expects users to hate; even so, he adds a button to jump to the actual page and one to turn the feature off. [when you break your streak as sort of a punishment it makes you guess]
- **consider** (patterns, all): Consider showing a crowd-sourced rating of a trait that helps users plan, such as a driver's chattiness rating with the number of reviews it is based on. Why: The presenter says this one could actually be pretty useful: riders can plan the trip and not feel awkward in silent moments. [Uber: there's this chattin rating based on 134 reviews]

### Decisions it informs

- Should an intrusive or opinionated feature be forced on everyone, or optional with an easy way to turn it off?
  - Forced, always on: Users cannot switch it off, or can escape only by paying or watching an ad, as with the YouTube watch-time notifications and the Gmail browser lock; the video presents these as infuriating. When: Never, according to the video's framing [inferred].
  - Optional, with a way out: The feature is switched on by choice and shows a notice, a button or a setting to turn it off, so even an annoying feature respects good UX. When: When a feature blocks, restricts or nags the user; the video does this for the Figma naming modal, the Apple Books streak punishment and the smart home pairing.
  - Recommendation: Give it a way out: the presenter adds a turn-off notice, button or setting because being annoying does not mean ignoring good UX.
- What should a one-click smart shortcut, like an auto-filled grocery cart, be based on?
  - Random picks: One click pulls up a random cart of goods; the presenter calls it entirely useless in practice. When: Only as a joke.
  - The user's past orders, with a cart total: The cart reflects what the user actually buys, and a visible total helps them keep on budget. When: When you want the shortcut to save real time.
  - Recommendation: Base it on past orders and add a cart total; that version might actually be sort of useful.

### Examples and visual references

- Usage-shaming watch-time message (YouTube): A brutally honest message about the excessive time spent on YouTube (342 videos is a lot), and the only choices are to subscribe to YouTube Premium or keep getting these notifications. It shows a guilt message paired with an upsell.
- Browsing-time pop-up (Amazon): After an exorbitant amount of browsing, a pop-up with a sassy message prompts the user to buy a book instead of spending so much time on Amazon.
- Browser lock-in with ad-gated access (Gmail and Google Drive): Gmail is shown as no longer available on Safari or any browser other than Chrome, with an option to watch a minute-long ad to get access. The same screen is applied to Google Drive.
- Step-goal guilt pop-up (A fitness app): A phone pop-up says you would need to run a marathon today to hit your goal of 10,000 steps a day for the week; a worse version adds a button suggesting you change the goal.
- Driver chattiness rating (Uber): While Uber looks for drivers, the driver pops up with a chattiness rating below, based on 134 reviews, saying he is a 3 out of five in chattiness, so riders can plan for silent moments. The presenter thinks this could be useful.
- Forced layer naming with a blocking modal (Figma): When switched on, new layers get a blank name; clicking anywhere except the name field brings up a modal that blocks everything until the layer is named, with a notice explaining how to turn the feature off.
- Randomized cart button (Instacart): One click fills a random cart of goods. The presenter says it would only be useful if based on past orders, ideally with a cart total.
- Pay for the person behind you at checkout (Instacart): A checkout option borrowed from drive-thrus, applied to orders that are usually 20 plus items for over $100.
- Streak-break punishment (Apple Books): Apple Books has a streak counter at the bottom; after a broken streak the reader drags a slider to guess where they were in the book, with a button to jump to the actual page and another to turn the feature off.
- Recipe behind a paywall or account wall (Yummly (named in timestamps)): A dinner recipe is restricted by a paywall or a make-an-account requirement, mirroring finance sites that charge to read articles.
- Neighbours pairing to your smart home devices (A smart home app): Other people can pair to your outlets and light bulbs and turn them on or off; the feature can be turned off in settings, and the presenter notes it would be far worse for smart locks.
