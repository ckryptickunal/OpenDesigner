## Summary

Kole Jain walks through three ways to price website design work: hourly, flat project pricing, and value-based pricing, which he calls the holy grail. He gives rough hourly rates by experience level (and notes they depend heavily on location), then shows how to build a project price from a break-even cost (estimated hours times hourly rate) marked up by a factor between 1 and 2 depending on how interesting and valuable the client is. Value-based pricing takes a share (5 to 10%) of the extra revenue a redesign produces, so prices scale with client size rather than time spent, but it only works when the result can be tied to a measurable revenue increase. This is freelance business advice rather than design guidance; for someone building a design system it matters mainly as a way to scope and price the work itself [inferred].

## Key Ideas

- There are three pricing models for design work: hourly, project (flat rate) and value-based.
- Hourly rates are very location specific; the video contrasts about $50 an hour in the US with about 12 in India.
- Hourly billing sets designer and client against each other: one wants more hours billed, the other fewer.
- Hourly pricing is useful only while you cannot yet price a project accurately.
- Clients prefer a hard number up front, which project pricing gives them.
- Project price starts from a break-even cost: estimated hours multiplied by your hourly rate.
- Time estimates should also account for meetings and revisions.
- Mark up the break-even by a factor between 1 and 2: close to 1 for a high-profile client who brings future work, higher for a dull client.
- Value-based pricing takes a cut of the extra revenue your redesign creates, generally 5 to 10%.
- Value-based pricing lets your price scale with client size regardless of hours spent, but requires estimating revenue gain and agreeing an ROI with the client.
- Value-based pricing does not fit clients with no revenue or non-profits; fall back to project pricing.
- Work usually takes longer than you expect, and your skills are worth more than you think.

### Decisions it informs

- How should a design project be priced?
  - Hourly: Bill for each hour worked; no concrete total, and designer and client pull in opposite directions on hours. When: When you cannot yet accurately price out a project, typically early in your career.
  - Project (flat rate): One fixed price the client knows up front; easier for the client, more estimating work for the designer. When: When you can estimate the hours, or as the fallback when value-based pricing does not apply.
  - Value-based: Price is a share of the extra revenue the work generates, so fees scale with client size regardless of hours. When: When the redesign can be tied to a quantifiable revenue increase and you can agree an acceptable ROI with the client.
  - Recommendation: Value-based pricing is presented as the holy grail because prices scale with larger clients irrespective of time spent; project pricing is the fallback when revenue gain cannot be measured; hourly is only useful until you can price projects accurately.
- How much should the break-even cost be marked up for a given client?
  - About 1x (break-even): Charge roughly your cost and not much more. When: A fantastic, big-name client who will likely bring you more clients.
  - 1.5x: A moderate markup, e.g. $4,000 becomes $6,000. When: A middle-of-the-road client who brings some value.
  - Up to 2x: The highest markup for the same amount of work. When: A really boring client; 2 is the top of the stated range.
  - Recommendation: Scale the factor between 1 and 2 by how interesting the project is and how much future value the client brings.

### Process

1. Pick an hourly rate: Base it on location and experience, e.g. $20 to $30 an hour for 1 to two years of designing in the US and similar countries, $35 to $50 after working with a few clients successfully, then 60 up to $100 after a year or so of that.
2. Estimate the time: Estimate how long the project will take using benchmarks as guidelines, and add time for meetings and revisions.
3. Compute break-even: Multiply the estimated hours by your hourly rate.
4. Weigh the client: Judge how interesting the project is and how much future value (reputation, referrals) the client brings.
5. Apply the markup: Multiply break-even by a factor between 1 and 2 to get the flat project price.
6. Check for value-based fit: If the work ties to a quantifiable revenue increase, estimate the revenue gain, agree an acceptable ROI with the client, and charge a share of the gain (generally 5 to 10%); otherwise keep the project price.
