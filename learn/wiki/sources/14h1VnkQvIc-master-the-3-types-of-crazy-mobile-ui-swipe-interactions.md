# Master the 3 Types of CRAZY Mobile UI Swipe Interactions

## Metadata

## Summary

Kole Jain sorts mobile swipe interactions into three groups: movement within a page, movement between pages, and swipe gestures that replace a button or reveal more context. Within a page, he takes swipeable scrolling cards from rigid to natural by opening up the easing curve for more bounce, skewing cards at the screen edge so they read as a 3D ring, and adding page indicators with a fluid, magnetic effect. Between pages, he argues transitions should follow the direction of the swipe and keep continuity, for example a card expanding to fill the screen or an image zooming in to become the next page's hero image, instead of sliding a new page in. For gestures, he recommends swipe-to-dismiss for popovers and pages, swipe-to-confirm sliders for high-impact or irreversible actions, and offering a button as well for people who don't know the gesture. For a design system, this gives a motion stance (almost never linear, the easing curve sets the tone), a set of mobile transition patterns, and the habit of pairing gestures with a visible button fallback.

## Key Ideas

- It's the interactions that turn a UI into an experience.
- Mobile swipe interactions fall into three groups: within-page navigation, between-page navigation and swipe gestures.
- Because a phone is much smaller than a desktop, its screen edges can be used for unique effects, such as skewing cards off the edge or shrinking items as they approach it.
- Swipeable scrolling cards beat stacking things vertically, but swiping through them feels rigid until the easing curve is opened up for more bounce, which lets them carry momentum.
- A linear ease is almost never right, because things in real life speed up and slow down.
- The easing curve sets the tone of the product: snappy and performant, fun and springy, or slow and smooth.
- Swipeable content needs indicators, and the indicator itself can be animated with a fluid, magnetic effect.
- Swipes are intuitive for people who know them, but a button should also be offered for people who don't (Gmail's swipe or tap to delete).
- Page transitions feel seamless when the new background and content move in the direction of the swipe.
- Continuity, such as an image zooming in to become the next page's hero image, beats sliding a new page in.
- Swipe-to-dismiss is quick and often intuitive for popovers and pages; zooming the background out and moving it down when a popover opens adds to the effect.
- A swipe-to-confirm slider is harder to trigger by accident than a button, so it suits high-impact or irreversible actions.
- Swipe gestures can replace a button or reveal more context; once users understand they can swipe, it is generally a faster, more seamless way to use the app.

### Process

1. Sort the swipe: Decide which of the three groups the interaction belongs to: within-page navigation, between-page navigation, or a swipe gesture that replaces a button or reveals context.
2. Fix the easing first: Starting from swipeable scrolling cards, replace the rigid curve with one that has more bounce so the cards carry momentum.
3. Add depth at the edges: Skew cards as they leave the screen edge, or shrink items as they approach it, so the content feels like a ring or spool under the finger.
4. Add indicators: Add page indicators under the cards, and animate them with a fluid, magnetic effect rather than a plain highlight.
5. Build a page change in the swipe direction: On swipe up: expand the card underneath to screen size, expand the tab behind it to fill the screen, fade the content up and in, then finish with an elastic snap at the top.
6. Build a circle wrap for step flows: Expand a circle very large while moving the content out, swap the circles, shrink the new one back down and move the new content in, following the direction of the swipe.
7. Give gestures a fallback: Keep a button (or possibly a long-press menu) for swipe actions, such as the buttons included on a swipe-dismissable popup.
