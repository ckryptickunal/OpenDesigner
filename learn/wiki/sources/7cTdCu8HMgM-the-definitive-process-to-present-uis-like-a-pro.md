# The DEFINITIVE process to present UIs like a pro

## Metadata

- Video ID: `7cTdCu8HMgM`
- Channel: Kole Jain
- Published: 2025-10-02T01:56:44Z
- URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7cTdCu8HMgM

## Summary

Kole Jain walks through eight ways to present one UI design: six creative 'showcase' techniques for portfolios and social media, and two professional techniques for client meetings. The showcase techniques build up from a plain, muted background with a faint shadow (or an accent-colored glow in dark mode), to letting the design extend off the frame, skewing it, popping one element out, arranging several screens into an ungridded collage, and pairing the UI with relevant photos. For clients he argues the goal changes from impressing them to building confidence, so he recommends device mockups (including custom ones made with AI image generation and a green screen) and working prototypes that show hidden interactions such as swipe actions and modal animations. For someone building a design system, this gives a checklist for how finished components and screens should be shown to stakeholders, and a clear split between showcase shots and client-facing presentation.

## Key Ideas

- Presentation is half of a design: the same UI looks better when it is presented well.
- Creative showcase techniques suit portfolios and social media; client meetings need realistic context instead.
- A plain, muted background is the safest showcase and works for nearly any design, because it lets the design itself pop.
- For a light-mode design, derive the background from the accent color by darkening and desaturating it, since a bright color would compete with the design.
- For a dark-mode design, large blurred circles of the accent color behind the UI create a soft ambient glow.
- Letting the design or its pattern extend off the frame ('exploding the image') makes it more visually interesting than a tidy crop.
- A slight skew draws attention; popping one part out with an offset and shadow draws even more.
- Multiple screens look better offset, slid off the page or arranged in a collage that deliberately does not line up in a grid.
- Collages need large designs such as landing pages; dashboards are better shown by zooming into one section and animating it.
- Photos in a showcase need to be relevant without distracting, and are often better for physical products; for software, fluid gradient images sometimes work.
- In client meetings the goal is to build confidence in the product, so mockups show the product where it will actually be used.
- In the creator's view, AI is not great at making thoughtful UIs but is good at making custom mockup scenes.
- Prototypes show rather than describe, and reveal hidden features such as swipe actions and animation sequences.
- Small details sell the whole experience, in a mockup scene just as in UI design.

### Decisions it informs

- Who is this presentation for?
  - Showcase (portfolio or social media): Creative compositions that aim to wow: backgrounds, glows, skews, collages and off-frame effects. When: Portfolios, Dribbble-style shots and social posts.
  - Professional (client meeting): Clear, realistic context through device mockups and working prototypes that build confidence in the product. When: Client meetings, where the client needs to understand the final product.
  - Recommendation: Match the technique to the audience: the source says showcase strategies won't work as well in client meetings, where the goal is confidence rather than wow.
- What background should a single-screen showcase use?
  - Plain muted color plus a faint shadow: Minimal; the design pops against a darkened, desaturated version of the accent color. When: Light-mode designs, and nearly any design as a safe default.
  - Accent-color glow: Large blurred accent circles behind the UI make it look like it is emitting soft light. When: Dark-mode designs.
  - Glassy background with a gradient stroke: Adds another layer of depth by defining the edges. When: When more depth is wanted than a plain background gives [inferred].
  - Recommendation: Don't underestimate minimalism: bland backgrounds make the design itself pop and work for nearly any design.
- How dynamic should the showcase composition be?
  - Contained and cropped: Tidy but less visually interesting. When: When a simple, minimal presentation is enough [inferred].
  - Exploding the image: Lines, patterns or the UI extend off the frame; a cool effect, though not practical. When: To add visual interest; can also extend brand visuals with key metrics.
  - Slight skew: Attracts attention to the UI (example: 2° vertical, -14° horizontal). When: When the UI needs more attention; keep the skew small.
  - Pop-out: One element is offset with a shadow, optionally with a gray placeholder left behind. When: When one part, like the dashboard, deserves even more attention.
- How should several screens be shown together?
  - Stacked: Pretty boring and doesn't work well. When: Not recommended.
  - Offset and slid off the page: Screens overlap and run off the edge. When: Works well, and suits mobile designs in the opposite orientation.
  - Ungridded collage: Fills the empty space with more screens; can add a few degrees of rotation, a skew, or pop out the most important frame such as the hero. When: Large designs such as landing pages.
  - Zoomed-in section, lightly animated: Looks both creative and professional. When: Dashboards, whose details are too fine for a collage.
  - Recommendation: Avoid stacking; use offsets or a collage for large designs, and zoom into a section for dashboards.
- What kind of mockup should a client see?
  - Simple device mockup: Even a plain computer does a lot to frame the design. When: A quick, realistic context.
  - Lifestyle mockup: The UI is shown being used on a device such as an iPad or Apple Watch, which elevates the design. When: To take it a step further.
  - Custom AI-generated mockup: A fully custom scene with the latest Apple device, made from a 30-word prompt in 2 minutes. When: When free mockups don't feel custom enough; also for e-commerce product mockups.
- How should interactions be shown to a client?
  - Static images or mockups: Lets the client see the experience, but hidden features and animations stay invisible. When: When there are no interactions worth showing [inferred].
  - Working prototype: Lets the client feel the experience and reveals hidden features like swipe actions, a modal animating in, or the sequence when a link is deleted. When: When interactions carry the polish, such as dashboard modals and swipe actions.
  - Recommendation: Don't describe, show: small interaction details communicate polish and thoughtfulness that static images can't.

### Process

1. Pick the setting: Decide whether the presentation is a creative showcase (portfolio, social media) or professional (client meeting).
2. Set a background: Light mode: darken and desaturate the accent color and add a faint shadow. Dark mode: add large blurred accent-color circles for a glow, or use a glassy background with a soft gradient stroke.
3. Add dynamic elements: Optionally let the design extend off the frame, skew it slightly (for example 2° vertical, -14° horizontal) and pop out the key element with an offset and shadow.
4. Arrange multiple screens: Offset and slide screens off the page, or build an ungridded collage with slight rotation; pop out the most important frame. For dashboards, zoom into one section and animate it.
5. Consider images: Add photos only if they are relevant and don't distract, mainly for physical products.
6. Generate a custom mockup with AI: Prompt for the latest Apple device with a green screen on its screen in a detailed scene (for example a MacBook Air on an orange faux leather padded chair, background blurred, soft shadows, moody lighting), punch out the green background, and skew the design in. Small details in the scene sell it.
7. Prototype the interactions: Build a working prototype to show hidden features like common swipe actions, a create-link modal animating in, or the animation sequence when a link is deleted.
