# The 4 Levels of Landing Page UI/UX Design

## Metadata

- Video ID: `eMMiLeo_UGI`
- Channel: Kole Jain
- Published: 2026-05-17T19:30:58Z
- URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eMMiLeo_UGI

## Summary

Kole Jain grades SaaS landing pages on four levels, from a template-like level one ("you'd be lucky to get $500" for it, 2 out of 10) to a crafted level four (well over $10,000, 10 out of 10), and walks each level through the same dimensions: layout, visuals, typography, copy, navigation, color, animation and page flow. The path runs from stock images and alternating text-and-image rows, through a stacked hero with the real product dashboard, to zoomed-in and then purpose-crafted product visuals, bento grids, clickable multi-selects, mega menus and copy that says how the product helps rather than what it does. He argues the jump from good to great is many small details, such as a blur effect on the click-through multi-select, a mega menu that slides content instead of closing and reopening, and a call to action that appears on hover only when needed. For a design system, it gives a ready audit checklist for marketing pages, a set of layout and navigation patterns with a clear order of maturity, and rules about where accent color should come from.

## Key Ideas

- A landing page can work functionally and still look like a template, because nobody made any design decisions.
- Alternating text-left, image-right rows all the way down reads as a template unless the page adds variety.
- Show the actual product instead of stock images; later, zoom in on its important parts, and finally craft visuals to show exactly what the product does.
- Letting color pops come from the product visuals is more vibrant and professional than spreading an accent color over everything that will take it.
- Navigation needs hierarchy: the sign-up action and the more important links should stand out, and the menu should be balanced rather than weighted to one side.
- Two buttons with the same label imply they go to the same place, which builds the right mental model.
- Copy should get shorter and punchier, and at the top level move from describing what the product does to how it helps.
- Swapping a row of cards for a bento grid, and a static card row for a clickable multi-select, adds room, interactivity and variety.
- At level three designers become product designers, adding brand elements such as a badge, a set of logos and a link to a social proof page.
- Good page flow means each section leads into the next instead of sections dropped one on top of another.
- Motion matures from none, to simple load animations, to smooth hover effects and fluid sliders, to small high-quality touches like a blur effect.
- The difference between good and great is almost never one big thing; it is attention to detail.
- A level four page needs no custom illustrations or 3D, only well-crafted UI with strong attention to detail.

### Rules and practices

- **should** (layout, web): Do not alternate text-left, image-right rows all the way down a landing page; vary the section layouts. Why: The alternating layout makes the page look like a template; professional sites that use it add good variety throughout. [text on the left, image on the right, alternating basically all the way down]
- **should** (layout, web): Use a stacked hero layout instead of a side-by-side hero. Why: It steps the page away from being a template and lets it breathe a little more. [much more modern stacked approach]
- **should** (patterns, web): Show the actual product (for example its dashboard) instead of stock images that have little to do with the product. Why: Unrelated stock images throw the whole page off; the product dashboard is a massive improvement. [literal stock image]
- **should** (patterns, web): Zoom in on the important parts of the product dashboard to curate exactly what the viewer sees, and at the highest level craft the visual to show exactly what the product does. Why: A full dashboard that is not perfectly framed does not draw attention to anything in particular; zooming in curates exactly what the viewer sees. [zoom in on the actual important parts of the dashboard]
- **should** (shape, all): Do not pair sharp-edged images with rounded-corner buttons on the same page. Why: The source calls it a disconnect that, with the level one page's other problems, means the page does not encourage you to keep scrolling. [disconnect between the sharp edges of the images and the rounded corners]
- **should** (typography, all): Use one font throughout the landing page. Why: The source calls using the same font throughout good even on the level one page. [we're using the same font throughout, which is good]
- **should** (typography, all): Build a clear typographic hierarchy through color and size. Why: Level one's weak hierarchy is one of its problems; level three makes a massive step forward with it. [clear hierarchy forming through the use of color and size]
- **consider** (typography, all): Do not let the headline dominate so much that the sub-line loses out. Why: The level three hierarchy is slightly overdone, so the headline dominates at the expense of the sub-line. [headline dominates at the expense of the sub-line]
- **consider** (typography, web): Use slightly looser type spacing and work with text widths so the lines lead into the content. Why: This is how level four balances its typography. [slightly looser spacing and work with the widths]
- **should** (content, all): Keep hero copy short and punchy; cut vague, long blocks of text. Why: No one stops to read a long block of text, especially once the visuals carry more of the narrative. [No one is stopping to read a block of text like this]
- **should** (content, all): Do not repeat the company name in the copy when it is already visible on screen. Why: The source says there is no need to mention it when it is already right there. [we don't need to mention the company name when it's right here]
- **should** (content, all): Write feature copy about how the product helps the user, not only what it does. Why: Moving from what the product does to how it inherently helps is much more valuable; it is the copy step from level three to level four. [transform from what it does to how it helps]
- **should** (components, web): Give the navigation menu hierarchy: make the sign-up action and the more important links (such as Product) stand out, for example with an outline on the most important buttons. Why: A flat menu where every item looks identical does not draw the eye to the most important action. [Every item looks virtually identical]
- **should** (components, web): Balance the navigation bar, for example by centering the secondary links, instead of weighting everything to the right. Why: A menu heavily weighted to the right does not balance the website well. [the other links are centered to balance that menu out]
- **should** (components, web): Give the hero call to action the same label as the top navigation call to action when both go to the same place. Why: Matching labels strongly imply the two buttons go to the same place, which is the correct mental model. [now these two have the same label]
- **consider** (components, web): Add small dropdowns on hover to the larger, more important navigation links. Why: Level two adds them as part of giving the menu hierarchy; later levels expand them into a mega menu. [small drop-downs on hover of the larger, more important links]
- **should** (color, all): Do not apply the accent color haphazardly to everything that will reasonably take it. Why: On level one, blue applied haphazardly makes the buttons dominate the text. [applied pretty haphazardly to anything that will reasonably take it]
- **should** (color, web): Do not rely largely on images to supply the page's color. Why: Level one pages lean on images for color because color is hard to nail, and it overwhelmingly misses the mark. [rely largely on images to do a lot of that heavy lifting]
- **should** (color, web): Let the color pops come from the product visuals, which allows neutral (white) buttons. Why: The source calls it a much more vibrant and professional way to add color, and it makes the swap to white buttons possible. [The color pops now come from the dashboard]
- **consider** (color, web): Thread color through the whole page using curated visuals, with images only where they make sense. Why: Zooming into the product can strip color out; curating the visuals lets color return across the page. [thread the color throughout the landing page expertly]
- **consider** (layout, web): Frame the page content with vertical lines that run the length of the page. Why: It structures the page and easily keeps it responsive on much larger screens. [frames in the content with vertical lines]
- **consider** (layout, web): Replace a plain row of four cards with a bento grid. Why: A bento grid gives the content a little more room to expand and breathe. [four cards to a Bento grid]
- **consider** (components, web): Replace a static three-card row with a multi-select the visitor can click through. Why: It adds interactivity and variety and leads to shorter, more compact sections. [three-card row to a multi-select]
- **consider** (patterns, web): Add brand-strengthening elements such as a badge, a set of logos and a button that takes visitors to a social proof page. Why: This is where designers stop being UI designers and become product designers. [adding in elements to strengthen the brand]
- **consider** (components, web): Use a mega menu for the most important navigation links. Why: It makes navigating easy and signals depth, that the product is worth exploring. [beautiful mega menu for the most important links]
- **should** (motion, web): When moving between mega menu items, keep the menu open and slide the content left or right instead of fully closing and reopening it. Why: Side by side, keeping it open is much smoother than a full close to a full open. [we keep it open and bump the content out to the left or right]
- **consider** (patterns, web): Instead of a permanent button under the logo section, show a call to action on hover only when it is needed. Why: The call to action appears only when it is needed; it is one of the level four details. [a call to action that appears only when it's needed]
- **consider** (motion, web): Polish the click-through multi-select with a blur effect (the transcript does not say exactly where the blur is applied or how strong it is). Why: The source says this simple, high-quality change takes the component from 8 out of 10 to 10 out of 10. [With that blur effect]
- **consider** (motion, web): Add subtle hover effects and interaction to the product visuals and bento tiles, and make sliders transition fluidly. Why: The source counts these subtle animations among the smaller details that make level three an 8 out of 10. [The dashboard now has some subtle hover effects]
- **consider** (motion, web): Add simple load animations rather than leaving the page static with only a basic hover effect. Why: Level one's lone hover effect does not make the site come to life; level two's load animations add much-needed movement. [simple load animations]
- **should** (layout, web): Design each section to lead into the next, for example fading out the dashboard to segue into the features, instead of stacking unrelated sections. Why: Level one feels like sections dropped one on top of the other; good flow encourages visitors to keep scrolling. [the fade out of the dashboard segues nicely]

### Process

1. Find your level: Grade the landing page on each dimension the video uses: layout, visuals, typography, copy, navigation, color, animation and page flow, and note which level each one is at.
2. Level one to two: Switch to a stacked hero, replace stock images with the real product dashboard, take color from the dashboard and use white buttons, tighten type, shorten the headline, give the menu hierarchy and balance, match the two call-to-action labels, and add simple load animations.
3. Level two to three: Zoom in on the important parts of the dashboard, frame the content with vertical lines, turn the four-card row into a bento grid and the three-card row into a multi-select, add brand elements (badge, logos, social proof link), add a mega menu, weave the accent back into the primary call to action with an icon, and smooth out hover effects and sliders.
4. Level three to four: Craft the visuals to show exactly what the product does, loosen the layout and the type spacing, rewrite copy from what it does to how it helps, thread color through the page, and polish motion details: a blur on the multi-select, a mega menu that slides instead of reopening, and a hover call to action on the logo section.
5. Check page flow: Walk the page top to bottom and make each section lead into the next, for example fading out the dashboard into the features, so visitors keep scrolling.
