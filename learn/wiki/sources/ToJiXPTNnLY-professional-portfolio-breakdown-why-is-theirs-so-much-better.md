# Professional Portfolio Breakdown — Why Is Theirs So Much Better?

## Metadata

- Video ID: `ToJiXPTNnLY`

## Summary

Kole Jain walks through professional designer portfolio websites and pulls out seven tips they share: a headshot above the fold, a concise intro, a simple site architecture, large and flashy imagery, visual case studies, a genuine about page, and personality. He shows a real portfolio for each tip and stresses that these are best practices, not rules every portfolio follows. The core argument is that visitors see before they read, so the work should lead and text should be secondary, while your face and story build trust and a touch of personality makes you memorable to potential clients. For someone building a design system this is mostly about a portfolio or personal landing page: page count, what goes above the fold, image-first layout and a voice with personality [inferred].

## Key Ideas

- Put your headshot somewhere on the site, ideally above the fold; a video of yourself goes a step further.
- Keep the intro concise: visitors want your name and what you do as soon as they land.
- A portfolio needs two, maybe three pages at most; a single page with links to its sections works when there is little content.
- Visitors see a portfolio before they read it, so large, full-width imagery should lead and text should be secondary.
- A good case study can be understood from its images alone, with a visual or GIF every couple of paragraphs.
- Ending a case study with what you learned and what you would improve is a good way to present that to potential clients.
- Case studies matter most when your field emphasises process, such as UX design, and less for graphic designers, copywriters or UI designers.
- An about page is the place to be genuine; sharing your face and story creates trustworthiness.
- Personality, even brutal honesty, makes a portfolio memorable and gives visitors something to relate to.
- These tips are best practices: several of the example portfolios skip some of them, it is a good idea to follow most or some of them, and you should do what works best for you.

### Rules and practices

- **should** (content, web): Place your headshot on the portfolio, ideally above the fold; a video of yourself is a step further if you are comfortable with it. Why: Being forthcoming with your face (and your story) creates a sense of trustworthiness that people gravitate to. [have your head shot somewhere in your website ideally above the fold]
- **should** (content, web): Open the portfolio with a concise intro that states your name and what you do, with no fluff or distracting pieces. Why: People want to know your name and what you do as soon as they land on the page. [the use of a very concise intro]
- **should** (layout, web): Limit the portfolio to two, maybe three pages at most, such as a homepage and a contact page, with an optional about page. Why: A simple site architecture avoids confusing pages and layouts; you don't need pages upon pages. Values: two maybe three pages maximum. [have a simple site architecture]
- **consider** (layout, web): Use a single-page layout with links to its sections when you do not have much content or want to keep things simple. Why: It makes sense when there isn't a lot of content, and visitors never have to leave the page. [many people even opt for a single page layout]
- **should** (layout, web): Lead with large, flashy imagery of your work (for example full-width images and bright colors, as in Luca Schultz's portfolio) and keep text secondary to the visuals. Why: Visitors see things before they read them, the imagery grabs their attention, and they come to see what you can build, not to read about it. [have large and flashy imagery]
- **should** (content, web): Add a visual or GIF every couple of paragraphs in a case study so the process can be understood from the images alone. Why: Being able to understand the process just by looking at the images is the key sign of a great case study. Values: every couple of paragraphs. [have visual case studies]
- **consider** (content, web): End each case study with what you learned and what you would improve in the future. Why: Designers often find halfway through a project that they would do something differently, and this presents that to people who might hire you. [what she learned from the case study and something she wants to improve]
- **should** (content, web): Include case studies when your area of expertise strongly emphasises the process of reaching a final solution, such as UX design; for graphic designers, copywriters or more UI-focused designers they are not necessarily needed. Why: The presenter says to absolutely include them where the process of how you get to a final solution is emphasised; otherwise there isn't as much need. [then I would say absolutely include them]
- **should** (content, web): If you include an about page, use it to be genuine and share your story, including honest personal updates, not only a list of work. Why: Honesty creates a real connection with potential clients and makes you more memorable. [the about page]
- **should** (content, web): Add a line of personality to the portfolio, such as what fuels you or an honest aside, so visitors have something to relate to. Why: Even visitors who don't fully agree will remember it and want to come back, and it makes people want to work with you. [add personality to your portfolio]
- **should** (layout, web): Do not build a portfolio with confusing pages and layouts or pages upon pages. Why: Confusing pages and layouts that don't make sense work against a simple site architecture, and a portfolio generally needs only two, maybe three pages. [not having confusing pages and layouts that don't make sense]
- **should** (content, web): Put content on the portfolio that reflects the kind of work you do; if your work is visual, show it visually. Why: Most designers produce something visual, and people come to a portfolio to see what you can build, not to read about it. [we want to put content on our portfolio that reflects the kind of work that we do]

### Decisions it informs

- How should you show yourself at the top of your portfolio?
  - A video of yourself: Goes a step above a photo: the person appears as a video instead of a still headshot. When: When you are comfortable being on video (Julia Fernandez's portfolio).
  - A headshot photo: Puts your face on the site, ideally above the fold; being forthcoming with your face builds trust. When: The default when a video is not comfortable.
  - No headshot: Stephanie's portfolio has no headshot and leads with her name and what she does; the source says that's okay. When: Acceptable; the source says not every portfolio follows every tip.
  - Recommendation: Have a headshot somewhere on the site, ideally above the fold; a video is a cool step further if you are comfortable with it.
- How many pages should the portfolio have?
  - Single page with section links: Links jump to different parts of the portfolio and the visitor never leaves the page. When: When you don't have much content or want to keep things simple (Minam's website).
  - Homepage and contact page: Two pages; the setup the presenter uses on his own portfolio. When: The common minimal setup.
  - Homepage, about page and contact page: Three pages, adding space to tell your story. When: When you want an about page, which the source calls totally fine.
  - Recommendation: Two, maybe three pages maximum; a single page is a great idea with less content. Avoid pages upon pages.
- Should your portfolio include case studies?
  - Include visual case studies: Shows how you reach a final solution, with a visual or GIF every couple of paragraphs and learnings at the end. When: When your field strongly emphasises process, such as UX design.
  - Skip case studies: The portfolio shows the finished work without process write-ups, as on the presenter's own portfolio. When: For graphic designers, copywriters or more UI-focused designers.
  - Recommendation: Include them if your area of expertise emphasises process (UX design is the best example); otherwise they are not necessarily needed.
- Should the portfolio have an about page, and what should it say?
  - A genuine about page with your story: Creates a real connection and trustworthiness; honest updates make you memorable (Lee Dave's website). When: When you choose to include an about page.
  - No about page: Keeps the site to a homepage and contact page. When: When you want the simplest architecture; the about page is optional.
  - Recommendation: Optional, but if you include it, be as genuine as possible; the source says sharing your story is well worth the time and effort.

### Process

1. Gather inspiration: Scroll through professional portfolios, such as the ones shown in the video, before working on your own.
2. Take a headshot: Hunt for a good spot to take the photo: a blank wall, by some bookshelves, or in front of your computer.
3. Structure a visual case study: Explain the process with a visual or GIF every couple of paragraphs, so it can be followed from the images alone, and finish with what you learned and what you would improve.
