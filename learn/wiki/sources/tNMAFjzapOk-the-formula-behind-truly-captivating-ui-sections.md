# The Formula Behind Truly Captivating UI Sections

## Metadata

- Video ID: `tNMAFjzapOk`
- Channel: Kole Jain
- Published: 2025-07-13T01:04:46Z
- URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tNMAFjzapOk

## Summary

Kole Jain gives a three-part formula for UI sections that feel captivating rather than chaotic: structure first, then motion, then a well-built surprise. Structure means restraint: a clear grid, generous space around text, blocks of text, simple illustrations and bold numbers, and making it obvious what the product is. Motion should draw attention to the product and support clarity, not exist for its own sake. Once structure and purposeful motion are in place, breaking the expected pattern once in a while (an unexpected hover effect, a page that unfolds, text that replaces the product view) makes a section engaging, as long as it is built well. He shows the same formula applies in web apps (a Linear menu) and mobile onboarding (Craft), where the direction of motion also tells users where they are.

## Key Ideas

- Captivating designs guide the eye intentionally; chaotic designs compete for attention, and most of the time they lose.
- A layout with too many components, text split into separate elements, pop-ups, small ornamentation and complex shapes feels crammed rather than crafted.
- Restraint in structure means a clear grid-driven layout with plenty of space given to text and surrounding elements.
- A quick test of structure: if you scrolled through the site for 5 seconds, could you understand the idea?
- If what the product is gets tucked in a corner at a tiny size and the imagery doesn't show the product, visitors can misread what the company does.
- Simplifying structure can strip personality; motion is where that personality comes back.
- Good motion draws attention to the product, for example a side image that morphs into the next section as you scroll.
- You don't need complex scroll animations: a single well-built hover animation that demonstrates the product can be enough.
- Complex animations added just to have them make navigation harder and add very little.
- Once in a while, break the expected layout pattern; unexpected moments are engaging when they are built well.
- A good feature-intro animation avoids excessive movement but is less boring than image-left, text-right.
- In app menus, alignment and typography differences can show what is clickable, what isn't, and what is a keyboard shortcut.
- A small surprise, like an expanding modal, rewards users for exploring a part of the UI.
- On mobile, onboarding is the best time to captivate users.
- The direction of motion situates the user: sliding up from the bottom signals a temporary action, and a screen sliding from the left shows progress in onboarding.
- The goal of a captivating UI is to make users feel that every scroll, click and hover makes perfect sense.

### Rules and practices

- **should** (layout, web): Test a page's structure by scrolling through it for 5 seconds: someone should be able to understand the idea in that time. Why: The structure should guide the user through the page; if the idea can't be grasped quickly, the layout isn't doing its job. Values: 5 seconds. [if you had 5 seconds to scroll through the website]
- **should** (layout, all): Don't cram a section with many components; use a clear grid-driven layout and give text and surrounding elements plenty of space. Why: Too many components make a layout feel crammed rather than crafted, while restraint guides the user through the page. [It has far too many components. The layout feels crammed.]
- **should** (layout, web): Present content as blocks of text, simple illustrations and bold numbers instead of splitting text into separate elements with pop-ups, small ornamentation and complex shapes. Why: The simpler version is easier to follow, even when both are reduced to wireframes. [Fiddle splits text into separate elements, has multiple pop-ups]
- **should** (content, web): Don't tuck the product's name or what it does in a corner at a tiny size such as a 12 pixel font; make it prominent. Why: Visitors may not realize what the product is; Micro reads like a wellness retreat ad rather than a project management tool. Values: 12 pixel font size. [tucked in the corner at a 12 pixel font size]
- **should** (patterns, web): Show an image of the actual product, such as its dashboard, near the top of a software landing page. Why: It makes the page read as a software company; showing it early also lets motion draw attention to the product right away. [grabbed an image of their dashboard]
- **consider** (motion, all): When simplifying a layout strips its personality, bring the personality back with motion. Why: After restructuring the Micro page, the unique design lost its personality, and motion is where it comes back. [that's exactly where Motion comes into play]
- **should** (motion, web): Use scroll motion that deliberately draws attention to the product, for example morphing a hero image into the next section or fading the text out while the dashboard fades in. Why: It gives users a great first glance at the product instead of motion for the sake of motion. [It wasn't motion for the sake of motion]
- **should** (motion, all): Don't add complex animations just to have them; every animation should support clarity. Why: Animations for their own sake make navigation more difficult and add very little to the site. [complex animations just for the sake of having complex animations]
- **should** (motion, web): Don't use animations that only look cool while delaying the product until further down the page. Why: Micro's animations are there to look cool but don't introduce the product early. [not introducing the product until even further down the page]
- **consider** (motion, web): For a simple landing page, consider one well-built hover animation that demonstrates the product instead of complex scroll animations. Why: Intercom shows a single-section landing page can be captivating this way; complex scroll animations aren't required. [you don't need complex scroll animations]
- **should** (patterns, web): Once in a while, break the expected layout pattern with one unexpected, well-built moment, such as a hover effect or a page that unfolds on click. Why: Unexpected things are engaging if they are built well; the typical layout would just scroll past the product and start talking about features. [once in a while, it's important to break the pattern]
- **consider** (motion, web): To introduce features after the product view, fade out the rest of the UI and move in simple text instead of switching to image-left, text-right. Why: It avoids excessive movement but isn't as boring as putting an image on the left and text on the right. [doesn't introduce excessive movement, but isn't as boring]
- **should** (components, all): In menus, use alignment and typography differences to show what is clickable, what isn't, and what is a keyboard shortcut. Why: Structure should tell the user everything they need to know, as in Linear's menu. [The alignment and typography differences show us what is clickable]

### Decisions it informs

- How much should a section hold, and how should its content be broken up?
  - Busy and ornamented: Many components, text split into separate elements, multiple pop-ups, small ornamentation and complex shapes; feels crammed and hard to follow. When: The source gives no case where this is right.
  - Restrained and grid-driven: A clear grid, generous space around text, blocks of text, simple illustrations and bold numbers; easy to follow even as a wireframe. When: Default for landing pages and sections that must explain a product quickly.
  - Recommendation: Restrained and grid-driven: the source says the difference comes down to which layout guides the user through the page, and then restores personality with motion.
- How should motion bring the product into a landing page?
  - Static image left, text right: Well structured but not exciting. When: The source calls it the boring baseline, not a recommendation.
  - Purposeful scroll animation: A hero image slides up, morphs or fades into the product view as the user scrolls, drawing attention to the product and giving a first glance at it. When: When the product can be shown visually, such as a dashboard.
  - Single well-built hover animation: One hover interaction demonstrates the product on a single-section page, without complex scrolling. When: Simpler pages, as with Intercom.
  - Complex animations for their own sake: Looks cool but makes navigation harder, adds little, and can delay showing the product. When: The source advises against it.
  - Recommendation: Use motion that supports clarity and points at the product, either a purposeful scroll animation or a single well-built hover animation; avoid complex animation for its own sake.
- How often should the design break the expected pattern with a surprise? (`Q-brand-04`)
  - Always standard: Predictable sections, such as scrolling past the dashboard and then listing features; clear but can feel boring. When: [inferred] When clarity matters far more than engagement.
  - Structured with an occasional well-built surprise: Mostly clear structure, plus one unexpected moment such as a hover effect with a screen-like background, a page that unfolds, or text replacing the product view. When: The source's recommended default for captivating sections, web apps and mobile onboarding.
  - Surprises everywhere: Many flashy elements and animations competing for attention; reads as chaotic. When: [inferred] Not recommended: the source's counter-examples of chaos (Fiddle Digital) and animation for its own sake (Capsules) point against it.
  - Recommendation: Structure first, then break the pattern once in a while with a well-built surprise; unexpected things are engaging only if they are built well.
- Which direction should a mobile element or screen move in?
  - Slide up from the bottom: Signals a temporary action, like Craft's editing tools. When: Temporary tools, sheets or actions.
  - Slide from the left: Shows progress through the onboarding flow. When: Moving forward through onboarding steps.
  - Recommendation: Pick the direction by meaning so the animation also situates the user.

### Process

1. Structure first: Cut components and ornamentation, use a clear grid, give text generous space, and use blocks of text, simple illustrations and bold numbers.
2. Check the structure: Wireframe the page with the content removed and see whether it is easy to follow; ask whether someone scrolling for 5 seconds would understand the idea.
3. Make the product obvious: Reorganize the text so the product name and purpose are prominent, fade the existing image out, and show the product itself (for example a dashboard image).
4. Add purposeful motion: Restore personality with motion that draws attention to the product: slide up the dashboard image and fade it out, then as the user scrolls, fade out the text and fade in the dashboard.
5. Break the pattern once: Where users expect the section to end and scroll away, add one well-built surprise, such as fading out the rest of the UI and moving in simple text that introduces broad features.
6. Apply the same order in apps: In web apps and mobile onboarding, use clear structure first, then motion, then a small surprise, and let the direction of motion situate the user.
