# Every UI/UX Concept Explained in Under 10 Minutes

## Summary

Kole Jain runs through the core UI concepts in under ten minutes: signifiers, visual hierarchy, grids and white space, typography, color, dark mode, shadows, icons and buttons, interaction states, micro interactions and text over images. Each concept comes with a quick before-and-after fix, such as turning a spreadsheet-like card into one with clear hierarchy, or tightening a loose headline. He gives concrete rules of thumb: a four-point grid, 32 pixels between items in a simple section, one sans serif font, headings tightened by -2% to -3% letter spacing with 110% to 120% line height, at most six font sizes on landing pages and normally nothing above 24 pixels on dashboards. He treats grids and hierarchy as guidelines rather than laws, and insists that color has a purpose and every interaction gets a response. For a design system, this is a compact list of beginner defaults for spacing, type, color, depth, button and input states, and feedback.

## Key Ideas

- Good UI uses signifiers (containers, selected states, grayed-out text, hover and press states, tooltips) so people know how it works without instructions.
- Hierarchy comes from contrast in size, position and color: the most important thing goes near the top, bigger and more colorful.
- Hierarchy is not an exact science; more than one layout can be right if the same ideas apply.
- A 12-column grid and exact 8-pixel spacing are guidelines, useful for structured, repeating pages but often ignored on custom landing pages.
- White space matters more than elaborate grids, and grouping elements that go together is another form of hierarchy.
- A four-point grid works because every value can be split in half, which keeps a design consistent.
- One sans serif font is enough for almost any design, and font choice shouldn't take much time.
- Tightening letter spacing and line height on large headings makes them look professional fast.
- The type scale depends on the product: landing pages can use up to six sizes with a large range, while dashboards normally stay at or below 24 pixels.
- Start color from one primary brand color, lighten it for backgrounds and darken it for text, which is already halfway to a color ramp.
- Color should carry meaning (semantic colors) rather than decorate.
- Dark mode needs its own adjustments: softer borders, cards lighter than the background for depth, and dimmer chips.
- Most shadows are too strong; cards need less than popovers, and a shadow shouldn't be the first thing you notice.
- Every user action needs a response: button and input states, loading spinners, success messages and micro interactions.
- Text over an image reads best with a linear gradient, optionally with a progressive blur, rather than a full-screen overlay.

### Process

1. Give a flat card hierarchy: Start with an image at the top; make the most important item large and bold at the top; put time and date below and much smaller; place the price at the top, right-aligned and blue; make the location smaller and below, using icons and alignment instead of 'from' and 'to'.

3. Tighten large headings: Reduce letter spacing by about -2% to -3% and drop line height to about 110% to 120%.
4. Build color from one primary: Pick the primary (usually the brand color), lighten it for a background, darken it for text colors, then extend into a color ramp for chips, states and charts. Add semantic colors for meaning.
5. Adapt a card for dark mode: Lower the contrast of the light border, make the card lighter than the background for depth, and dim the chip's saturation and brightness while flipping that for its text.
6. Tune shadows: Reduce opacity and increase blur; use less for cards and more for popovers; check that the shadow is not the first thing you notice.
7. Size icons with text: Take the text's line height (24 pixels in the example), make the icons the same size, then tighten up the text.
