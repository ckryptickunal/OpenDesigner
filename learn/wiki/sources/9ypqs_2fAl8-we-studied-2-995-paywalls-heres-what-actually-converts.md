# We Studied 2,995 Paywalls. Here’s What Actually Converts.

## Metadata

- Video ID: `9ypqs_2fAl8`
- Channel: Mobbin
- Published: 2026-07-09T07:35:18Z

## Summary

Mobbin studied nearly 3,000 paywalls and hundreds of subscription flows, and interviewed Jonathan Parra, who has designed over 4,700 paywalls, to find what separates high-performing paywalls from average ones. The main lessons are that a paywall is a flow, not a single screen: it should sell the outcome first, appear at the right moment in the customer lifecycle, and reduce the fear of being charged with trial timelines, reassuring subtitles and longer trials. Some findings run against intuition: added friction such as requiring a credit card can raise paid conversion, and an ugly, text-heavy paywall sometimes beats a beautiful one, so there is no universal best paywall, only better experiments. It also covers pricing and packaging (default to yearly, show only two plans), framing techniques (social proof, value framing, price anchoring), formats (multi-page, table, video) and the tactics to be wary of, such as spin-the-wheel offers, fake urgency and free-trial toggles that Apple began rejecting. For a design system this gives concrete patterns, copy and component choices for subscription screens, plus a warning that conversion patterns must be tested and weighed against long-term trust, retention and LTV.

## Key Ideas

- A paywall is a flow, not a screen: the product should sell the outcome before it asks for money, so the paywall feels like the natural next step.
- Users sometimes decide to pay before they even see the paywall, so every touchpoint in the lifecycle matters.
- Paywalls can appear after onboarding, when unlocking a premium feature, inside settings, or as a win-back offer before the user leaves.
- A well-designed paywall shown at the wrong moment is still a bad paywall.
- Multi-page paywalls, where information unfolds gradually, almost always beat single-page paywalls in Jonathan's experience.
- Some of the best-performing paywalls reduce perceived risk: a step-by-step trial timeline, a 'no commitment, cancel anytime' subtitle, or a longer trial.
- Presentation can change results even when the offer does not change.
- More friction can raise paid conversion: requiring a credit card for a trial filtered out people who were never serious.
- Common framing techniques are social proof, value framing matched to what users care about, and price anchoring.
- Default to a yearly plan for the highest LTV, show only two plans, and hide any others behind a 'view all plans' sheet.
- Tables show what users miss if they don't subscribe; video can communicate value faster than screenshots or bullet points.
- Polish (animation, custom characters, world building) is becoming a differentiator now that anyone can ship an app.
- Spin-the-wheel paywalls and fake urgency can maximise short-term revenue but wear down trust; users learn to close paywalls to get an offer.
- There is no universal best paywall; radically different design tests move the needle most.
- Retention and LTV matter more than getting the first payment.

### Process

1. Map the touchpoints: Consider the whole customer lifecycle: after onboarding, premium-feature unlocks, settings and win-back offers before leaving.
2. Sell the outcome first: Before the paywall, show the user their plan and the ideal outcome they will get, so the paywall feels like the natural next step.
3. Reduce risk: Add a trial timeline, a 'no commitment, cancel anytime' subtitle, or a longer trial instead of a discount.
4. Set pricing and packaging: Default to yearly, show two plans, and put others behind a 'view all plans' sheet.
5. Ship a simple base paywall: Get users onto it and measure base stats before optimising different placements.
6. Test radically different designs: Pit video, bullet list, trial timeline and long-form paywalls against each other until one blows everything out of the water.
7. Refine copy, pricing and flow: Keep testing copy, pricing and flow until something clearly wins.
8. Watch retention and LTV: Judge the result by retention and LTV, not only by how many people subscribe.
