# Redesigning A Modern Skincare UI from SCRATCH (+ free design files)

## Metadata

- Video ID: `BvbFPzLjWcU`

## Summary

Kole Jain takes a skincare website from Dribbble, critiques it element by element, and redesigns its desktop hero into a split-screen beauty landing page. He removes elements that do not earn their place (social links, a page outline on a short site, a written testimonial in the header, decorative shapes), fixes the layout first, and resizes the design to a real browser viewport of 1920 pixels across by about 1,000 pixels tall instead of the taller canvas Dribbble shots use. He then reworks the nav around a logo placed front and center, turns product photos into a card slider, fixes the hero's contrast by choosing a better photo and adding a subtle overlay, reuses the brand font (Apple's New York) from the logo and packaging, and adds subtext with a primary and a secondary call to action. For a design system, it shows a critique-then-rebuild process for marketing heroes, a realistic viewport check, and the habit of reusing brand typography across logo, packaging and UI. He openly says parts of the result favour looks over usability, so those choices are taste, not rules.

## Key Ideas

- A skincare website should lead with great product photos, a bright white background and lots of white space.
- New and interesting layouts are welcome, but like everything in design they can be taken too far.
- Critique the existing design element by element before changing anything, keeping what works (the top nav and its icons).
- When elements are everywhere with no clear layout, fix the layout first and remove what is not needed.
- An ultrawide layout that splits the screen into two halves is common on modern beauty websites.
- Design a desktop hero for a real browser viewport: about 1920 by 1,000 pixels, not a tall Dribbble canvas that hides what would be cut off.
- Simplify a nav by collapsing secondary links into a menu button, turning the cart into an icon and the profile into 'sign in'.
- When there is not enough room to show every product, turn the product cards into a slider.
- Fix hero contrast by first choosing a better photo (right vibe, nothing in the way, good lighting), then adding a subtle overlay if it is still a little bright.
- Reuse the font already on the logo and packaging for the website heading; he 'may as well use it here too'.
- He finishes the hero with subtext plus a primary and a secondary call to action.
- Use ready-made product mockups instead of making product renders from scratch.
- He finds beauty packaging has too much stuff on it from an aesthetic standpoint; for a minimal look he wants less on the pack.

### Process

1. Critique the original: Go element by element: keep what works (top nav, fitting icons) and note what doesn't (social links, site outline, cut-out image, header testimonial, decorative shapes, copy like free shipping over $500).
2. Get product and brand assets: Find matching product mockups (minimalmockups.com) and use Photoshop to make the product look good; make a logo and packaging, looking at real packaging (L'Oreal) and aiming for minimal and bougie.
3. Fix the layout first: Remove unneeded elements (social links, testimonial, site outline, decorative shapes) and pick a clear structure: an ultrawide two-half split with image and text on the left, products on the right.
4. Resize to a real viewport: Squish the design to 1920 pixels across by about 1,000 pixels tall to match a full-screen browser on most monitors.
5. Rework the nav: Put the logo front and center, collapse favorites and notifications into a menu button, make the cart an icon, turn profile into 'sign in', and add a search bar for balance.
6. Replace product photos with cards: Build a product card (decide which corners hold the buttons), turn the cards into a slider and add a 'new arrivals' chip.
7. Fix image contrast: Search for a hero image with the right vibe, no clutter and good lighting; add a subtle overlay if it is still bright.
8. Apply brand typography: Use the logo and packaging font (New York) for the hero heading.
9. Add copy and calls to action: Add subtext, a primary and a secondary call to action, and (for looks) an arrow-only button that leads to the full product line.
