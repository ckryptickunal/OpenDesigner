# Developing Taste

## Summary

Emil Kowalski argues that once software that simply works is easy to ship (especially with AI), what sets a product apart is its brand, design, intuitiveness and overall experience, which he sums up as taste. He defines good taste not as personal preference but as a trained instinct for recognising what elevates a design. The essay gives a three-part method to train it: surround yourself with great work from a curated list of respected tastemakers, rationalise why something feels great instead of labelling it good or bad, and practise the craft while seeking critique from the right people. For a design system this sets the process bar: work is judged against the best existing products, preferences must be explained with reasons rather than gut feel, and early imperfect output is expected on the way to quality [inferred].

## Key Ideas

- When a product category becomes commoditised, quality and details become the differentiator; software has reached that point.
- A product that merely works is no longer enough because people expect things to work and anyone can build that, especially with AI.
- Brand, design, intuitiveness and the overall experience are what make a product stand out.
- Taste is a trained instinct, not personal preference: the ability to see beyond the obvious and recognise what elevates a design.
- Train taste by exposing yourself to great work in your own field.
- Build a curated list of tastemakers: find respected people, then look at whom they admire, and study their work.
- Replace good-or-bad labels with reasons: explain why something feels great and look for patterns instead of trusting gut feel alone.
- Study apps rather than just using them, asking why a specific interaction feels good.
- Practise the craft by making things; practice turns you into a good judge of taste and, over time, a tastemaker.
- Critique from the right person speeds up learning more than trial and error alone.
- Early work falling short of your own standard is a good sign that your taste is ahead of your skill; keep going.

### Decisions it informs

- Where should the quality bar for a design come from: great work by respected people, or personal preference? [inferred: applies the essay to the reference step] (`Q-ref-01`)
  - Great work from a curated list of respected tastemakers: You learn how greatness looks and feels and can bring the best things into your own work. When: The source's recommended way to train taste.
  - Personal preference or gut feel alone: Things get labelled good or bad without understanding why; the source says taste is commonly mistaken for personal preference. When: The source advises against relying purely on intuition.
  - Recommendation: Point to the designs and apps of people respected in their field (and the people they admire), then say why each one feels great instead of labelling it good or bad.

### Process

1. Surround yourself with great work: Find people respected in the field, look at whom they admire, and build a curated list of tastemakers. Look at their designs, use their apps and read their books so you learn how greatness looks and feels.
2. Think about why you like something: Do not label things good or bad. Rationalise why something feels great, analyse the patterns, and study apps rather than just using them by asking why a specific interaction feels good. Go beyond the surface and stay curious.
3. Practise and seek critique: Create things in your discipline. Ask the right person for feedback, which accelerates learning more than trial and error alone. Expect early work to fall short of your taste and keep going; it improves over time.
