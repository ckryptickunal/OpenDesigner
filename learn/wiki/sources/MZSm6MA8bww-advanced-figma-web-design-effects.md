# Advanced FIGMA Web Design Effects

## Summary

Kole Jain walks through four decorative Figma effects built from gradients, blend modes, inner shadows and blurs: a kaleidoscope effect, a clay effect, a metallic effect and plugin-free mesh gradients. For each he names where it fits: kaleidoscope for credit cards, stickers and buttons; clay for isometrics and illustrations that need depth; metallic for credit cards and metal mockups; mesh gradients for websites, posters and slideshows. The recipes hinge on a few load-bearing details, such as keeping the Screen layer on top in the kaleidoscope, avoiding saturated colors for clay, and needing a noticeable color change in the middle of the metallic gradient. For a design system, the video is a recipe book for decorative surfaces and brand illustration rather than for core UI, and he openly says the kaleidoscope's usability is poor even though it looks good.

## Key Ideas

- Decorative effects in Figma can be built from layered gradients, blend modes (Difference, Screen, Overlay), inner shadows and blurs; only the kaleidoscope's optional noise step uses a plugin.
- Each effect has named best uses: kaleidoscope for credit cards, stickers and buttons; clay for isometrics and illustrations; metallic for credit cards and metal mockups and 'not a whole lot else'; mesh gradients on websites, posters, slideshows and 'just about everything else'.
- In the kaleidoscope, layer order is load-bearing: the Screen layer must sit on top of everything else or the effect breaks.
- The kaleidoscope's angular gradient should alternate black and white an even number of times.
- An effect can look good while being poor for usability; the kaleidoscope with overlay text is his own example.
- The clay effect comes from two inner shadows, one covering the top and right edges and one the bottom and left, and it works poorly with saturated colors.
- A metallic look needs a noticeable color change in the middle of the gradient; the edge colors matter less.
- A white radial gradient on Overlay adds depth and thickness to a metallic surface.
- A layer with a fill of 20% in a red, orange or blue turns plain metal into rose gold, gold, bronze or titanium.
- Mesh gradients come from stacked, blurred, semi-transparent circles on Overlay; the more overlapping layers, the brighter and more vibrant the result.
- Mesh gradients are more art than science: iterate on colors, blurs and transparencies until it looks right.

### Decisions it informs

- Which decorative effect fits this surface?
  - Kaleidoscope: Striped radial pattern from the angular gradient, with noise and overlay text; looks good, but its usability is poor. When: Credit cards, stickers and buttons.
  - Clay: Muted-color shape with inner shadows on opposite edges that gives it depth. When: Isometrics or illustrations that need some depth.
  - Metallic: Gray and dark blue sheen with a highlight and inner shadows that reads as a metal plate; can be tinted to rose gold, gold, bronze or titanium. When: Credit cards and metal mockups, and not much else.
  - Mesh gradient: Soft, blurred multi-color background that grows brighter and more vibrant with more layers. When: Websites, posters, slideshows and most other surfaces.
  - Recommendation: He calls the mesh gradient probably the most useful of the four and uses it on just about everything he can; the others have narrower use cases.
- Should a metallic surface stay plain metal or be tinted?
  - Plain metal: Grays and dark blues with an Overlay highlight and inner shadows. When: The default metallic credit card or mockup.
  - Tinted metal: An extra layer with a 20% fill in red, orange or blue gives a rose gold, gold, bronze or titanium feel. When: When you want a specific finish such as rose gold.
- How vibrant should a mesh gradient be?
  - Few overlapping layers: Softer and less bright. [inferred] When: A calmer background. [inferred]
  - 8 to 10 or more overlapping layers: Brighter and more vibrant. When: When you want a brighter, more vibrant gradient.
  - Recommendation: Don't be afraid to use 8 to 10 layers or more, then tune colors, blurs and transparencies until you're happy.

### Process

1. Kaleidoscope: base: Draw a circle with a radial gradient in any colors.
2. Kaleidoscope: stripes: Add a layer with an angular gradient alternating black and white an even number of times (eight; 10 or six also work) and set it to Difference.
3. Kaleidoscope: screen layer: Duplicate the stripe layer, set the copy to Screen and keep it on top of everything else.
4. Kaleidoscope: finish: Add noise with the Noise & Texture plugin, then add thicker text set to Overlay.
5. Clay: Draw a rectangle in a non-saturated color, add one inner shadow covering the top and right edges and another covering the bottom and left edges, and optionally a drop shadow.
6. Metallic: Draw a rounded rectangle with a linear gradient of mostly grays and dark blues with a noticeable change in the middle, add a white radial gradient layer on Overlay, then two inner shadows.
7. Metallic: tint: For rose gold, gold, bronze or titanium, add another layer with a 20% fill in a red, orange or blue.
8. Mesh gradient: Place a semi-transparent, blurred circle as the background, add more semi-transparent, blurred circles set to Overlay, repeat (8 to 10 layers or more), and tune colors, blurs and transparencies.
