# The one thing vibe coding CAN’T fix about your website

## Metadata

- Video ID: `RCneB_MQ7qs`
- Channel: Kole Jain
- Published: 2026-08-19T14:34:37Z
- URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RCneB_MQ7qs

## Summary

Kole Jain tears down a viewer's restaurant management software website that is clean and functional but feels soulless, then redesigns it from visual identity through layout to animation. His diagnosis is that the site says it is for restaurant owners but never looks like it has heard of a restaurant: it has no images at all, which he reads as a sign of vibe coding. He builds a visual identity from a single moody image, pulls an earthy palette and accents from it, swaps a monospace font for a serif paired with the existing sans, and adds feature icons. He then 'Frankensteins' a wireframe from sections of other sites and fills each section with the same repeatable recipe of background, noise texture, type and scattered images. Finally, he gives the site an animation identity of motion themes that repeat, that direct the eye, and that soften hard breaks between sections. For a design system, the lesson is to define layout and visual identity carefully so different identities can be swapped into the same layout, and to choose imagery, type and motion for the feeling that fits the brand and its audience rather than from a formula.

## Key Ideas

- A site can be clean, compact and well animated yet still feel soulless if it does not relate to the people it is for.
- A website with no images anywhere feels robotic; he calls it a telltale sign the site was vibe coded.
- A strong site relates to its audience visually (laptops and keyboards for freelancers) and has one actionable heading that promises what the product does.
- Start the visual identity from one moody image instead of bright, sterile light modes or flat, typical dark modes.
- Pull the base palette from the tones in the mood images, then add a few accent colors.
- Pairing a serif with the existing sans adds personality (like a printed menu); the monospace font did not fit.
- The identity should come from what conveys the feeling for the brand, not from a formula.
- 'Frankensteining' means picking sections from other sites to work out the message and page flow, then keeping only the wireframe.
- Once the wireframe and identity exist, building each section becomes almost formulaic: background, texture, text, images.
- Things that look scattered and random are actually ordered: larger elements below, smaller on top, with a safe margin around text.
- Because the layout and the visual identity were both carefully defined, different identities can be swapped into the same layout for very different feelings.
- Animation is usually an afterthought, but repeated motion themes can form an animation identity for the brand.
- Motion should direct the eye to the focal point and smooth hard breaks between sections.
- When several images all matter, rotating them on a timer encourages people to see every one.

### Rules and practices

- **should** (content, web): Show images that relate to the target audience on a product site; do not ship a site with no images at all. Why: A site with no images anywhere feels robotic, and the source calls it a telltale sign of vibe coding; images are how the site relates to its users and builds trust. [no images anywhere]
- **should** (content, web): Use a single, actionable hero heading that promises exactly what the software does. Why: It is one of the things that makes Actual relate to its users and build trust. [a single, very actionable heading]
- **consider** (process, all): Start the visual identity from one moody image: crop it, darken it, add noise and put the brand name on it, instead of starting from a bright light mode or a flat dark mode; then gather more images with the same moody vibe. Why: It gets away from bright, sterile light modes and flat, typical dark modes, and the site's feeling starts from the visual identity. [start with a single image that's more moody]
- **consider** (color, all): Pull the base color palette from the tones in the brand's mood images, then add a few accent colors. Why: It keeps the palette tied to the feeling of the images; accents give extra colors to play with. [pulling out some of those earthy tones from the images themselves]
- **consider** (typography, all): Pair a serif with the sans to add personality when the brand calls for it, instead of a monospace that does not fit. Why: The serif adds personality, like a printed menu for a restaurant brand; the monospace was not working. [mix in a serif font to add a little more personality]
- **consider** (components, all): Draw feature icons in the brand's accent colors so each icon stands for one main feature of the product. Why: The icons come to represent the main features of the product, and the same icons from the brand deck are reused in the features section. [Using our accents, I'm going to add in a few icons]
- **should** (process, all): Do not derive the visual identity from a formula; choose each element for the feeling it conveys and whether it makes sense for the brand. Why: It is all about what conveys the feeling and makes sense for the brand; he calls this one of the most important things about building the identity. [none of this came from a formula]
- **consider** (process, web): Work out the page's message and flow by assembling a wireframe from sections of reference sites, then keep only the wireframe and discard their visuals. Why: It creates a site flow that resonates, while the look comes from your own identity. [Frankensteining a site to work out our message and site flow]
- **should** (patterns, web): Add extra social proof, such as testimonials, beyond a logo ribbon. Why: Logos are nice, but it is typically good to have a little extra social proof. [typically it's good to have a little extra social proof]
- **consider** (layout, web): In feature sections, make the images bigger and cut most of the text. Why: He borrows Miro's full-screen color tabs but wants the images to dominate the page. Values: 90%. [cut like 90% of this text]
- **consider** (color, web): Add subtle noise to image or color backgrounds behind text. Why: It adds texture and also helps the contrast of the text. [a little bit of noise, which will also help with contrast for the text]
- **should** (layout, all): Place hero buttons below the subtext at a gap at least twice the gap between the heading and the subtext. Why: The large heading already has a good amount of distance between the baseline where the letters sit and the actual bottom of the heading. Values: at least twice the distance of the heading and subtext. [at least twice the distance of the heading and subtext]
- **should** (layout, all): When scattering image assets around text, put larger elements below and smaller elements on top, and always keep a safe margin around the text. Why: Things should feel scattered and random while actually being ordered. [larger elements below, smaller elements on top]
- **consider** (color, web): Darken the edges of the hero to draw attention to the center. Why: It really draws attention to the center where the text sits. [darken the edges]
- **consider** (color, web): Match each section's background and texture to the feeling it needs, for example a cream background with no noise and a graph-paper texture for a clean software feel. Why: It lets a section lean into the clean software feel while staying in the identity. [a cream background with no noise]
- **should** (motion, web): Define a few motion themes and repeat them throughout the site, instead of one-off effects. Why: Themes that repeat, rather than happening once, form the basis of an animation visual identity and make motion feel part of the brand. [they don't just happen once, but are repeated throughout the website]
- **should** (motion, all): Use motion to direct the eye to each section's focal point; on the hero, the focal point is the text. Why: When applying motion to any design, the aim is to direct the eye and the focal point. [directing the eye and the focal point]
- **consider** (motion, web): Soften a hard break between the hero and the next section: slide the hero elements off screen and blur the text while the new section slides over the top; apply the same idea in reverse on the CTA section. Why: It makes the hero feel like part of the background and the change smoother than a hard break. [slide the elements off the screen and blur our text]
- **consider** (motion, web): When several feature images all matter, rotate them automatically on a timer and add a slight zoom on hover. Why: It encourages the user to check them all out and brings a dry middle section to life. [have them on a timer rotating automatically]
- **consider** (content, all): Add texture images to the identity that fit the darker colors and the audience, for example a counter with pots and pans and measuring spoons, or a tablet to stay closer to the software theme. Why: With the darker colors he wants some texture in the identity. [With these darker colors, I want to get a bit of texture in here]
- **consider** (layout, web): In a how-it-works section, make it less step-like and use much bigger background images that show who would use the product, with stronger noise and mid-size text. Why: The section should talk about how the product works while the background images reinforce who would use it. [really use those background images to reinforce who would use our product]

  - Typical dark mode: Can feel flat and typical. When: Not recommended here; he wants to get away from it.
  - One moody image: A cropped, darkened image with noise and the brand name sets a feeling that the palette, textures and fonts then follow. When: When the site needs to feel like it belongs to its audience and stand out.
  - Recommendation: Start from a single moody image, because it escapes the sterile light and flat dark defaults and sets the feeling first.
- Should the site show images, and what kind? (`Q-img-01`)

  - Images that relate to the audience: Restaurant items, kitchen counters and tablets (or laptops and keyboards for freelancers) make the site feel built for its users and build trust. When: Whenever the site says who it is for.
  - Recommendation: Use images that relate to the audience, because that is how the site relates to users and builds trust.
- Use one type style or pair a serif with the sans? (`Q-type-05`)

  - Sans with a monospace: The original site's choice; he says it was not working. When: Not recommended for this brand.

- What background should each section use?

### Process

1. Diagnose the feeling: Compare the site with sites that clearly relate to their audience and find the gap, for example saying who it is for but never showing it.
2. Set the mood with one image: Pick one moody image, crop it, darken it, layer on noise and add the brand name; then gather more images with the same vibe.
3. Build the palette: Pull earthy tones from the images for a simple palette and take a few accent colors from another site (Anchor, as heard).
4. Add texture images: Add textures that fit the darker colors, such as a counter with pots and pans or, closer to software, a tablet.
5. Choose fonts: Keep what works (the sans), replace what doesn't (the monospace) and mix in a serif for personality.
6. Make feature icons and finish the deck: Draw icons in the accent colors for the main features, then add more images and the logo mark.
7. Frankenstein the wireframe: Choose a reference for each section (hero, logo ribbon, how it works, features, testimonials, CTA, footer), note what to change, then keep only the wireframe.
8. Build each section with the same recipe: Background (brand color or image), texture (noise level), text (serif and sans split, subtext, buttons with the right spacing), image assets in a hidden order, then darkened edges.
9. Repeat across sections: Apply the same recipe to each wireframe section, varying background and texture to fit its purpose.
10. Add animation last, as identity: Define repeated motion themes, direct the eye to focal points, smooth hard section breaks, and bring dry sections to life with rotation and hover zoom.
