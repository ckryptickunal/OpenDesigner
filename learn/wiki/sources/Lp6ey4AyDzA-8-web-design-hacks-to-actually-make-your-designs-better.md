# 8 Web Design Hacks To ACTUALLY Make Your Designs Better

## Metadata

- Video ID: `Lp6ey4AyDzA`

## Summary

Kole Jain gives eight quick fixes for web designs. Text hierarchy should come from font weight and text color as well as size, with at most two weights and two text colors (the primary color plus a 45 to 70% opacity version). Space should separate content instead of cards and dividers, icons should come from one consistent pack, muted colors (off-whites in light mode, bluish blacks in dark mode) beat pure saturated ones, and a header should have only one primary call to action. He also recommends giant display text as a visual element, dropping gradients and shadows that aren't working, a drop-shadow recipe, and previewing designs on the screen they are made for. For a design system, these become concrete defaults for text weights and colors, grouping by space, icon sets, neutral tints, button hierarchy and shadow tokens [inferred].

## Key Ideas

- Hierarchy is the thing designers most often get wrong, and size is only one of three tools for it; weight and color are the other two.
- Too many font weights or text colors looks as bad as too many font sizes, so cap each at two.
- A second text color can be the primary text color at reduced opacity, somewhere between 45 and 70%.
- Many separators and containers make a design feel cluttered even when there is little in it.
- Cards take up a much larger footprint, especially in multi-column layouts like an e-commerce store; space alone can separate content.
- People read things that are close together as related and things far apart as unrelated.
- Icons from one pack or library share a style; hunting icons one at a time on a mixed-style site makes matching hard.
- Simpler icons are generally better, and detail should scale with icon size.
- Beginners treat color as all or nothing; subtle, muted colors such as off-whites and bluish blacks signal a more experienced designer.
- Not every button needs a background: a header should have one primary call to action, and secondary buttons can drop their fill.
- In a delete dialog the destructive action is the primary one and gets the fill, preferably red; cancel is demoted.
- A good display font can be as visually important as an image, but giant text should appear only once or twice per page.
- Gradients and drop shadows often detract; if they aren't working, removing them can make a site feel more professional.
- Designing zoomed out in Figma leads to oversized fonts and spacing, so preview on the real target screen.

### Rules and practices

- **should** (typography, all): Build text hierarchy with font weight and text color as well as size, instead of relying on size alone. Why: Most people use size exclusively; weight and color add more differentiation and drastically improve a design without changing much else. [we can use font weights and font colors to create more differentiation]
- **should** (typography, all): Use at most two font weights in a design. Why: Having too many font weights, like too many sizes, is not visually appealing. Values: two font weights. [at most I like to use two font weights]
- **should** (typography, all): When a font family offers many weights, pick two that are separated by at least one weight step. Why: The two weights need to be distinct enough to create differentiation [inferred]. Values: separated by at least one weight. [make sure the two you choose are separated by at least one weight]
- **should** (color, all): Use at most two text colors: the primary text color (black or white) and that same color at 45 to 70% opacity. Why: Too many text colors is not visually appealing; the reduced-opacity color creates hierarchy. Values: two, 45 and 70%. [anywhere between 45 and 70% is good depending on the circumstance]
- **should** (layout, all): Separate content with space instead of dividers and cards, especially in multi-column layouts such as an e-commerce store. Why: Removing dividers and cards lets everything breathe, and the added space is what separates each piece of content. [removing the dividers and cards lets everything breathe more]
- **should** (layout, all): Don't pile on separators and containers; bento-style sections are fine, but many of them make a design feel cluttered. Why: Many separators and containers make a design feel cluttered even when there is little in it. [with many separators and containers your design can feel cluttered]
- **should** (layout, all): Place related items close together and unrelated items farther apart so spacing shows grouping. Why: People intrinsically read distance as unrelated and closeness as related. [things that are farther away aren't related and things that are closer together are]
- **should** (components, all): Take icons from one icon pack or library rather than searching a mixed-style icon site for each one. Why: Mixed-style sites like Flaticon make it very hard to find multiple icons styled the same way. [using icon packs and libraries is a great way to get around the different styles]
- **should** (components, all): Keep icons simple, and match detail to size: more detail for large icons, less for small ones. Why: You don't need a piece of art as an icon; generally the simpler the better. [the larger the icon the more detail it needs]
- **should** (color, all): Incorporate off-whites into light mode and bluish blacks into dark mode rather than only pure white and pure black. Why: It is a clear indication of a designer who has been designing longer; beginners treat color as all or nothing (pure saturated colors plus black and white). [incorporating off- whites into your light mode and bluish blacks into your dark mode]
- **should** (color, all): Don't be afraid of muted colors such as baby blue, beige and lavender, especially when adding one more color beyond an AI or auto-generated palette; don't limit color to pure saturated hues plus black and white. Why: Getting better with color means being aware of subtle, muted colors, and finding just the right extra color requires experience and knowledge. [don't be afraid of muted colors they're your friend]
- **should** (components, all): When several buttons sit close together, give a background only to the primary action; not every button needs one. Why: Buttons that are equally important draw just as much attention and compete; removing or reducing the others' backgrounds manipulates the hierarchy. [this mostly pertains to multiple buttons being in close proximity to one another]
- **must** (patterns, all): Give a header section only one primary call to action. Why: Two equally important buttons draw just as much attention and compete. Values: one primary call to action. [only ever have one primary call to action]
- **should** (components, all): If a header must keep a second button, remove its background so it no longer competes with the primary one; otherwise drop the second button. Why: Removing the background manipulates the hierarchy so the buttons are no longer equally prominent. [the first is to remove the background completely]
- **should** (components, all): In a delete dialog, give the delete button the primary fill, preferably red, and demote cancel with no background, only a border, or reduced opacity. Why: Delete is the primary action despite being destructive, and the demoted cancel button then doesn't compete with it. Values: red. [it should get the primary color preferably red]
- **consider** (typography, web): Treat large display type as a visual element, and pick a good display font for it. Why: A really good display font can be just as visually important as an image. [use text as a visual element and grab a display font]
- **must** (typography, web): Don't use giant text more than once or twice on a page. Why: He says not to overdo giant text: once or maybe twice per page is enough (no further reason given). Values: once or maybe twice in a page. [you certainly shouldn't do it more than than that]
- **should** (elevation, all): Review gradients and drop shadows honestly, and remove them entirely if they aren't working. Why: They more often than not detract from the design, and removing them often makes a site feel more professional. [if it isn't working with them just remove them entirely]
- **should** (elevation, all): Keep a drop shadow's x offset less than or equal to its y offset. Why: No reason given beyond it being his basic drop-shadow strategy for shadows that are hard to simply remove. Values: x value should always be less than or equal to your y value. ### Process

1. Set text hierarchy: Start from size, then add two font weights separated by at least one weight step and two text colors (primary plus 45 to 70% opacity); compare before and after.
2. Strip extra containers: Remove dividers and cards that aren't needed and let the added space separate content, keeping related items close.
3. Pick one icon set: Choose an icon pack or library (his go-to is Feather Icons in Figma), keep icons simple, and match detail to icon size.
4. Review colors for muted options: Swap pure white and black for off-whites and bluish blacks, and look at muted colors such as baby blue, beige and lavender.
5. Audit button groups: Where buttons sit close together, keep one primary action; discard or remove the background from the others, and in delete dialogs make delete the red primary and demote cancel.
6. Find a display font: Filter Google Fonts for display fonts and scroll; use giant text once or maybe twice per page.
7. Honest review of gradients and shadows: Remove them if they aren't working; for shadows that stay, keep x at or below y, blur at 1.3 to 2 times y, and opacity at 15 to 20% instead of Figma's 25% preset.
8. Preview on the target screen: Preview on desktop or mirror the prototype with the Figma app, and leave room for the browser bar on mobile and desktop.
