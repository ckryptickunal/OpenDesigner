# I Studied 2,108 Dashboards To See What Sticks

## Metadata

- Video ID: `kdRkuqu8apE`

## Summary

Mobbin reviewed 2,108 dashboards and argues that the basics (clear hierarchy, good spacing, useful charts) make a dashboard fine but not memorable. The dashboards that stick are one step ahead: they put key insights and next steps next to the data, package stats into recaps and highlights, and pick visuals that make the data make sense instantly, such as a real-time view where every dot is a real visitor, a heat map, or a clean layout when most dashboards look busy. On mobile, standout dashboards turn personal data into shareable formats like a swipeable story, and some products coin their own metric (streaks, a readiness score, closing rings) that keeps users loyal. AI is filling the 'why did this change?' gap, from a single prompt under a chart to a full AI layer beside the data. For a design system, it gives a dashboard stance: design for understanding and next action, leave things out, and test each screen with the question 'Would I screenshot this, and why?'

## Key Ideas

- Clear hierarchy, good spacing and useful charts make a dashboard fine, but not memorable.
- Memorable dashboards are one step ahead: they show what to do about the data, not only what happened.
- Key insights placed next to a chart turn a plain line chart into guidance.
- Recaps, daily briefs and post-activity highlights package data so it is easy to understand and feels like an accomplishment.
- Standout dashboards pick visuals that fit the data, such as a real-time view where every dot is a real visitor or a heat map where darker means busier.
- Most dashboards look busy, so a very clean one stands out.
- Small moments of delight, like a cake inside a donut chart or a game instead of an empty table, show the dashboard was not a template.
- The word dashboard began as a carriage's mud board and became the place for signals in cars; the form keeps changing, but the job never changed.
- When products do roughly the same thing, the dashboard does a lot of the convincing: it shows what the product does, how you are doing and what to do next.
- On mobile, data can be presented like content, for example a swipeable story that people want to share.
- Coining your own metric (streaks, readiness score, closing rings) makes the metric part of how users talk and makes switching unlikely.
- AI is filling the gap of explaining why a number changed, from a single prompt below a chart to a full AI layer beside the data.
- The author's gut check for a dashboard is: would I screenshot this, and why?
- The dashboards worth screenshotting were not the prettiest; they knew what to leave out.

### Decisions it informs

- Should the dashboard show only the data, or also tell people what it means and what to do?
  - Data only: A chart shows what happened; the dashboard works but is easy to forget. When: The source does not recommend it; it describes most dashboards this way.
  - Insights next to the chart: Key insights sit beside the line chart and show what to do about it (Faith). When: When users need to act on the data.
  - Recaps and briefs: A weekly recap and a daily brief package the data so it is easy to understand (Origin); post-activity highlights give a sense of accomplishment (Runner). When: When data accumulates over time or after an activity.
  - Recommendation: Go one step ahead of the data: show what to do about it, not only what happened, because those are the dashboards people remember.
- How should the dashboard look so it stands out instead of looking like a template?

### Process

1. Cover the basics: Get clear hierarchy, good spacing and useful charts in place; they make a dashboard fine but not memorable.
2. Ask how users will get it: For each piece of data, ask how to make it make sense to your users so they get it instantly, instead of using a template.
3. Add the next step: Show how the user is doing and what to do next, for example insights beside a chart, a recap, a brief or highlights.
4. Leave things out: Remove what does not help; the dashboards worth screenshotting knew what to leave out.
5. Screenshot gut check: Ask 'Would I screenshot this, and why?' before calling the dashboard done.
