# The stupid simple way to learn UI/UX design in exactly 10 minutes

## Metadata

- Video ID: `9WVt1CelBfg`
- Channel: Kole Jain
- Published: 2026-07-18T13:58:53Z

## Summary

Kole Jain lays out how he would learn UI/UX design from scratch, ending in a 28-day plan. He first trains the design eye by pulling apart one landing page (the Superpower site) into four zones and pointing out the exact spacing, word counts and opacity that create its grouping and hierarchy. He then argues that learners should copy designs exactly rather than redesign them, shows the handful of hero layout options and says the choice depends on what the content is trying to do. He separates UI (the flavor: the same layout restyled as warm and friendly or modern and premium) from UX (the thinking behind a flow such as an onboarding that branches on team size). For a design system this gives concrete grouping and hierarchy values, a way to show one layout in two personalities as a visual sample, and a model for branching product flows [inferred].

## Key Ideas

- Landing pages that look different often share one skeleton: main text block, navbar, stats and image.
- Spacing creates grouping: the closer two elements sit, the more closely people associate them.
- Short hero copy communicates fastest; the example uses seven words in the heading and 14 below.
- A high-quality image makes or breaks a landing page; keep its focal point clear and its background calm.
- Hierarchy can come from color and opacity, not only from font size.
- To learn, copy a design exactly instead of redesigning it, so you are forced to notice the small details.
- At any point there are only a handful of layout options; the right one depends on what the content is trying to do.
- UI is the flavor of a design; the same layout can feel friendly or premium through styling alone.
- UX (product design) is about the user's journey, not spacing or typography.
- Even a simple onboarding hides many decisions, such as whether to block on email verification and whether to branch by team size.
- Do not start a portfolio while learning, because your taste will start to change over the next one to two months.
- The 28-day plan moves from training the eye, to cloning designs, to restyling (UI), to designing a branching flow (UX).

### Rules and practices

- **should** (layout, web): In a hero text block, put the subtext closest to the heading (8 px), the eyebrow slightly farther (12 px) and the buttons farthest (32 px). Why: The spacing makes people read heading and subtext as one group, then the eyebrow, then the buttons. Values: 8 pixels, 12 pixels, 32 pixels. [The subtext is only 8 pixels away]
- **should** (content, web): Keep hero copy very short, as in the example's seven-word heading and 14-word subtext. Why: It communicates the message as quickly as possible. Values: seven words, 14. [It's only seven words in the header]
- **consider** (components, web): Keep the navbar small and out of the way; in the example its most prominent item is a 'Become a member' call to action [inferred]. Why: It stays out of your way, like most modern navs. [stays out of your way just like most modern navs]
- **should** (content, web): Use a high-quality image asset on a landing page. Why: The source says a high-quality asset makes or breaks most landing pages. [makes or breaks most landing pages]
- **should** (layout, web): Keep the hero image's focal point unobstructed: place text, nav and stats around it (in the example, above, below or to the left, leaving the center and right open). Why: Everything sits around the focal point, leaving the center and right wide open for the imagery. [the focal point of the image is never obstructed]
- **should** (content, web): Do not use a hero image with a busy background behind or next to the text. Why: A busy image makes things a little more chaotic and the text harder to read; with one image the text on the left is basically gone. [the background of the image isn't too busy]
- **should** (typography, all): Create hierarchy with color as well as size: for example, set two lines at the same size and lower the secondary line's opacity. Why: The lower-opacity line blends into the background and does not jump out as much. Values: 15 pixels, 55% opacity. [using color, not just size, to create some hierarchy]
- **should** (layout, all): Choose a layout by first asking what the content is trying to do. Why: All the hero layout decisions come down to that one thing. [what is the content trying to do]
- **consider** (layout, web): For a profound statement, center the text, increase the font size and give it a lot of space. Why: Layout decisions come down to what the content is trying to do, and a profound statement needs a lot of space to breathe. [if you're trying to have a profound statement]
- **consider** (layout, web): When a features list has more items than fit on the screen, show them in a bento grid. Why: The user should see all the features, and nothing is lost as they scroll down. [swap this out for a bento grid]
- **consider** (shape, all): To make a product feel friendly and welcoming, use a warm background, colorful visuals, rounded corners and large, bubbly buttons. Why: Styling changes how the same layout feels (its flavor). [make this feel very friendly and welcoming]
- **consider** (shape, all): To make a product feel modern and professional, use much less rounded corners, less colorful and more premium imagery, and optionally add noise to the background. Why: Styling changes how the same layout feels (its flavor). [more modern and professional]
- **should** (patterns, all): Give sign-up screens an escape hatch link to the login page. Why: Many people end up on the sign-up page unintentionally. [escape hatch to the login page]
- **consider** (patterns, all): Ask for team size during workspace creation and branch onboarding on it: teams get an invite-members step, individuals skip it. Why: Team size is important because it shapes how the next onboarding steps function. [we split into two flows]
- **should** (process, all): When learning, copy an existing design exactly instead of redesigning it, measuring details like card spacing, nav height and button padding. Why: Copying forces you to notice the small details, and after a few sites the layout patterns emerge. [don't redesign it. Literally copy it]
- **should** (process, all): Before designing in Figma, pull apart a site you like into its zones, spacing, text length and hierarchy. Why: You start to see the decisions behind the design, and proximity, contrast, consistency and hierarchy become easier to recognise. [before you ever even get into Figma]
- **must** (process, all): Do not start a portfolio while you are still learning design. Why: The presenter calls it the biggest mistake a learner could make, because taste will start to change over the next one to two months. Values: one to two months. [the biggest mistake that you could make]
- **consider** (layout, web): Keep the hero's main text block from dominating the page, and set the spacing around it intentionally. Why: The source points to this as one of the design details to notice in a good landing page. [doesn't dominate the page]
- **consider** (layout, web): In a features list, let the visuals lead and use the text only to support them. Why: Layout follows what the content is trying to do, and a features list is about the visuals. [the text is really just there to support the visuals]
- **should** (process, all): When learning, build a swipe file of 20 or so inspiring hero and feature sections, then annotate a couple for spacing, hierarchy and what is grouped with what. Why: Week one of the plan is all about training your eye. Values: 20 or so. [Start by building yourself a swipe file]

### Process

1. Deconstruct a design: Pick any site you like and break it into zones (main text block, navbar, stats, image); note spacing, word counts, what is grouped with what, and how hierarchy is made.
2. Build a swipe file: Week one: collect 20 or so hero and feature sections that inspire you from Mobbin, Dribbble, Pinterest or elsewhere.
3. Annotate: Pick a couple from the swipe file and annotate spacing, hierarchy and what is grouped with what.
4. Clone a hero exactly: Pick one design you love from the swipe file and clone its hero section exactly.
5. Clone its feature section: Clone the feature section from the same site.
6. Clone a different site: Pick a second site with a totally different layout and clone it too.
7. Practise UI flavor: Restyle one of the layouts you built twice: once warm and friendly, once modern and premium.
8. Practise UX: Design the two screens where the onboarding journey splits: an invite-your-team page for the manager and a setup page for the solo user.
9. Hold off on the portfolio: Keep focusing on the skill rather than a portfolio while your taste develops.
