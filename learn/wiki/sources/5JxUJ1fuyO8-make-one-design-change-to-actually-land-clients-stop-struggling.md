# Make ONE Design Change to Actually Land Clients (Stop Struggling)

## Metadata

## Summary

Kole Jain argues that designers struggle to land clients because they sell good-looking websites instead of solving the business problem underneath, which is usually a need for more customers, sales or sign-ups. He contrasts what clients judge (mostly looks, the UI) with what users and results depend on (functionality, the UX), using a boutique store whose real fix was better search and product categories. He suggests adding related services that fit the client's situation, presenting past work as short case studies with numbers, and building the site yourself so you can read the analytics before and after a redesign. He closes by saying looks and usefulness are not a trade-off, citing the aesthetic-usability effect. For someone building a design system, the lesson is to start from the goal the screens must serve and let that drive layout and content choices before styling [inferred].

## Key Ideas

- The need behind "I need a website" is usually more customers, clients or sales.
- Clients mostly judge a site on looks, but its users care far more about whether it works.
- Describe the problem you solve, not only the website you will deliver.
- Think about the work more from a marketing standpoint than a creative one.
- Getting results needs more focus on UX, such as search and product categories, even though clients care more about UI.
- A plain page can succeed if the information is clearly laid out and the next click is obvious.
- Niching on a problem, not an industry, lets you help clients in any industry that has that problem.
- Choose add-on services that match the client's situation, such as conversion work for a site that already gets lots of traffic.
- Pick page sections for what they achieve: testimonials bring an agency clients, an impact list brings a nonprofit donations.
- Show past work as a case study: services, the problem, and the solution with numbers.
- Building the site as well as designing it gives you the analytics to check whether a redesign had an impact, before and after.
- Small pre-launch UX tests often have too few people to be significant.
- Good looks and usability support each other: attractive designs are perceived as easier to use.
- The end goal is keeping visitors on the site longer and driving more sales or sign-ups, in addition to a beautiful design.

### Rules and practices

- **should** (process, web): Before designing, name the client's underlying business problem (usually more customers, clients, sales or sign-ups), not just the request for a website. Why: People buy because a product solves their problem, and a site that looks good but serves no purpose still won't sell. [most business owners have a pretty consistent need]
- **should** (process, web): Design for the site's end users and their tasks, not for what the client likes the look of. Why: The client judges mostly on looks, but the users of the site care much more about functionality. [building a product for the end user not the client]
- **should** (patterns, web): When a store has so many products that customers struggle to navigate, fix it with a refined search system and recategorized products to make things more intuitive, treating it as a UX problem rather than a visual update. Why: The difficulty navigating is the real problem and presumably costs sales; the fix is UX, not UI. [a refined search system and recategorizing her products]
- **should** (layout, web): Lay out pages so all the information is clearly laid out and it is obvious where to click next. Why: ClickFunnels and similar sites work so well, even though they are not particularly beautiful, because of this clarity. [do I know where to click next]
- **consider** (content, web): Choose homepage sections by the goal they serve, for example a testimonial section for an agency or a list of the last year's good work for a nonprofit. Why: Both are clear choices for getting more clients or donations for each organization. [deciding to have a testimonial section displayed on the homepage]
- **should** (process, web): For a redesign of a site that already gets heavy traffic, offer conversion rate optimization rather than SEO as the add-on service. Why: With a site already getting so many clicks, a marginal gain in sales at that scale makes the client a ton of extra money, so the client would likely pay extra for it; extra SEO is never a bad thing but is not the clear choice here. [you can either offer SEO or conversion rate optimization]
- **should** (process, all): Don't present past work as only the finished website; show that it solved the client's problem. Why: As you move from designing websites to solving problems, just displaying the website is no longer enough to sell future clients. [just displaying the website is no longer enough]
- **should** (process, all): Write each case study as: the services provided, the problem, then the solution with numerical evidence whenever possible. Why: Little case studies about how you achieved your results are what really sell your services to future clients, and presenting undeniable results is the single best sales pitch. [a super brief outline from our boutique store]

### Process

1. Find the real problem: Look past "they need a website" to the business need, usually more customers, sales or sign-ups, and what is blocking it (for the boutique: too many products and hard navigation).
2. Frame the solution as UX: Describe the fix in terms of the problem, such as a refined search system and recategorized products, rather than "a website with a CMS and modern design".
3. Add related services: Offer services that help the core problem and fit the situation: SEO, conversion rate optimization, social media content, blog posts, email marketing, paid marketing, or a design retainer.
4. Build it yourself: Design and develop the site (in code or a website builder) so the client has one contact and you keep analytics access.
5. Measure before and after: Use the site's analytics to compare before and after the new design, staying on as admin or with view-only permissions.
6. Write a short case study: List the services provided, define the problem (often losing customers before they convert), then present the solution with numerical evidence whenever possible.
